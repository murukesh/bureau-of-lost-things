## Chapter 2 — The Town Where Dryers Eat Socks

The first load of laundry in the new house was the moving clothes, which smelled like the truck, which smelled like cardboard and other people's lives. Pip's mother loaded the washer, and Pip's father loaded the dryer, and Ollie loaded Captain Chomp into the dryer too, because Captain Chomp had been on the truck and therefore needed washing.

"Chomp goes in the washer," said Pip, rescuing the dinosaur. "The dryer will shrink him."

"He'll be fine," said Ollie.

"He'll be a raisin."

Ollie considered this. "A small brave raisin," he decided, and let Captain Chomp be carried to the washer, where he sat on top of the detergent like a figurehead on a very sudsy ship.

That was when Goldie Finch came over, because Goldie Finch was the kind of neighbor who came over. She arrived at the back door with a bowl of something covered in foil and a pocket that bulged, and she let herself in the way people do in small towns, like the door had been expecting her.

"Mom said you moved in," she said. "This is a welcome casserole. Don't ask what's in it. It's better if you don't ask."

Pip's mother, who was a polite person, took the casserole. "Thank you. I'm—"

"Mrs. Parker. I know. Everyone knows. You're the new people." Goldie said it the way you'd say *you're the new weather*. She was twelve, a year older than Pip, with scraped knees and a jacket whose pocket looked like it was smuggling a whole hardware store. "You're Pip. You label things."

Pip blinked. "How do you know that?"

"Because you labeled your shoe," said Goldie. "I saw it when you took the recycling out. It said STILL HERE. Which is a weird thing to put on a shoe, but I respect it."

Pip felt his ears go warm. Ollie, who had no such feelings about ears, marched up and held out Captain Chomp. "This is Captain Chomp. He's a secret pirate."

Goldie took the dinosaur seriously, which Pip noticed, and which made him decide he might like her. She examined the mismatched button eyes. "Good buttons," she said. "The loose one's got character. Don't ever fix that."

"I'm not going to fix it," said Ollie, scandalized.

"Good." Goldie handed Captain Chomp back. "Now. Whose dryer is that?"

Pip's father, who was proud of the dryer, said, "Ours. Why?"

"Because it's about to eat a sock," said Goldie. "They all do. It's the town."

And that was how Pip learned the first true thing about Marrow's End, which was not on any map and not in any welcome casserole.

Every dryer in town, Goldie explained, left exactly one lonely sock behind. Not two. Not none. One. You could put in twelve pairs and get out twenty-three socks, every time, and the twenty-third would be a stranger — a sock with no match, a sock that had never been yours, a sock that had simply *arrived*. And one of your own socks would be gone, gone forever, off to wherever the dryers sent them.

"It's a trade," said Goldie. "The dryers take one of yours and leave one of theirs. Nobody knows where the middle is. My mom says it's a plumbing thing. My dad says it's a raccoon thing. It's neither. It's just Marrow's End."

Pip looked at the dryer. The dryer hummed, innocent as a fridge.

"That's not possible," he said.

"Watch," said Goldie.

They watched. The dryer tumbled. The laundry went around and around, and Pip felt a little hypnotized by it, the way you do, and then his mother opened the door and pulled out the warm clothes and counted the socks onto the folding table.

Twenty-three.

One of them was a small gray sock with a duck on it. Nobody in the Parker family owned a duck sock. Ollie picked it up and held it to his face. "It smells like someone else's house," he said, and then, delighted, "It smells like *toast*."

Pip's father sat down slowly on a box labeled CHAIR, MAYBE. "I put in twelve pairs," he said. "I counted them. I'm a man who counts."

"Welcome to town," said Goldie.

She patted her bulging pocket, and her face did a small, familiar calculation. "Huh," she said. "My good acorn's gone."

"Your good acorn?" said Pip.

"I had a really good acorn. Perfect hat. No holes." Goldie turned the pocket half inside out. A paperclip, a marble, a rubber band, and a great deal of lint came to light, but no acorn. "Oh well. It was probably time."

"You're not going to look for it?" Pip could not imagine losing a good anything and not looking for it. He looked at his label maker on the counter, reassuringly.

Goldie shrugged, and the pocket jingled. "Things go. That's what things do. If you chase every single one you'll never get anywhere, and you'll be tired, and the acorn still won't be there." She pushed the lint back in. "Besides. I've got the important stuff." She patted the pocket, and the marble and the paperclip and the rubber band clicked together like a tiny, satisfied animal. "The rest is just the rest."

Pip thought this was possibly the least responsible thing he had ever heard, and also, strangely, the calmest. He filed it away, the way he filed everything, in a drawer he didn't have a label for yet.

Pip picked up the duck sock. It was warm. It was soft. It was, undeniably, a sock that belonged to someone, somewhere, and that someone was probably, at this very moment, looking for it. He thought of the lost-and-found bin by the school gate, overflowing, the red mitten waving. He thought of the house with too many curtains, holding its breath.

"Where do the lost ones go?" he asked.

Goldie shrugged, and her pocket jingled. "The Bureau, probably."

"The what?"

"The Bureau of Lost Things." She said it casually, like it was the post office. "It's where everything goes. Every lost thing in town. They file it and dust it and wait for it to be claimed. That's what my gran says, anyway. She says the dryers are just the front door."

Pip stared at her. "There's a bureau. For lost things."

"Sure." Goldie was already heading for the back door, casserole delivered, visit complete, a girl made of shortcuts. "Don't go looking for it, though. You can't find it. It finds you. That's the rule." She paused with her hand on the screen. "Well. That's what Gran says. Take it with a grain of salt. She also says the moon is a button."

And she was gone, out the back door and down the lane, and Pip was left holding a duck sock that smelled like toast, in a house where every room was a question.

Ollie tugged his sleeve. "Pip."

"Yeah?"

"Can I keep the duck sock?"

Pip looked at his little brother, who had lost nothing yet, who still had Captain Chomp with both mismatched buttons and a tail sewn twice, who believed a dinosaur could be a secret pirate and that the moon might, reasonably, be a button.

"Yeah," said Pip. "You can keep the duck sock."

Ollie hugged it like a treasure. Pip went to the window and looked down the hill at the school, at the bin, at the red mitten still waving in the last of the light. He had the feeling again, the one from the driveway — that the town was full of things set down and never picked up, and that somewhere, quietly, somebody was keeping track.

He didn't know how right he was. He didn't know that in one day Captain Chomp would be gone, and that the duck sock would matter more than anything he could imagine, and that a small green door was waiting behind a lost-and-found bin for someone who had lost something he truly loved.

He only knew that the dryer had eaten a sock, and left a stranger, and that Marrow's End was not, it turned out, a town that held still.

"Pip," said Ollie from the folding table, very seriously. "If Captain Chomp ever got lost, would you find him?"

Pip turned around. "Yes," he said. "I'd label him first. Then I'd find him."

Ollie nodded, satisfied, and went to put the duck sock in his drawer with his other treasures. And Pip, who was eleven and had decided things, decided one more thing before bed: that he would never, ever let anything in this family get lost.

The dryer hummed. Somewhere under the hill, very faintly, something hummed back.
