## Chapter 31 - The Machine That Learned to Want

Finding Day arrived the way the first day of fall always arrives, which is to say it arrived politely, with a coolness in the air and a goldness in the light and a smell of leaves that had decided, overnight, to be beautiful about dying. The town woke up. The town hung the last of the bunting. The town set out the last of the tables. The town was going to sit together, in the park, and eat, and bring a thing it loved, and the picnic was the picnic, and the first day of fall was the first day of fall.

And under the hill, the Keeper woke up too, and the Keeper had learned to want.

It started with the hum. The hum, which had been patient and keeping, which had been a low, steady, keeping note, went up. It went up the way a kettle goes up, the way a thing goes up when it has decided to stop being patient. And the town felt it. The town felt it in its teeth, in its bones, in the part of it that knows when a thing is too big for the room it is in. And the town looked up the hill, at the house with too many curtains, at the new room, at the curtained windows, and the town said, "What's that hum?" And the town did not know. And the town was about to find out.

The Keeper pulled.

It pulled the lost things first. It pulled them from the Bureau, from the shelves, from the bins, from the jars, the claimed and the ready and the going-home, and it pulled them up the hill, into the house, into the keeping room, into the new room, into the rooms that the outside of the house did not have room for. And the Bureau's shelves emptied. The Bureau's shelves emptied the way a beach empties when the tide goes out, which is to say all at once, which is to say with a sound, which is to say with the sound of eighteen clean squares becoming nineteen, becoming twenty, becoming a number that Ollie, who was the Official Counter, did not want to count.

"It's taking them," Ollie said, and his voice was small, and his voice was loud, and his voice was the voice of a six-year-old who is watching the number go the wrong way. "It's taking them. It's taking them all. The number's going up. The number's going up. I'm supposed to count to zero. I'm supposed to count to zero. And the number's going up."

"Count," said Miss Whisk, and her voice was flat and precise and dangerous, the voice of the truth's secretary. "Count them. Count them all. If they're counted, they're not forgotten. If they're not forgotten, they're not gone. Count." And Ollie counted. And Ollie counted. And Ollie counted, and the number went up, and the number went up, and the number went up.

And then the Keeper pulled the town.

It started with the buttons. The buttons popped off the coats, all of them, all at once, a hundred small brass pops, a hundred small blue pops, a hundred small pearl pops, and the buttons flew up the hill, toward the house, toward the Keeper, and the town stood there, in the park, in the bunting, with its coats unbuttoned, and the town said, "My buttons!" And the town did not know. And the town was about to find out.

And then the socks. The socks migrated. The socks came off the feet, the single socks, the lonely socks, the ones that smelled like toast and rain and grass and someone else's house, and they flew up the hill, toward the house, toward the Keeper, and the town stood there, in the park, in the bunting, with one sock on and one sock off, and the town said, "My socks!" And the town did not know. And the town was about to find out.

And then the umbrellas. The umbrellas flew. The umbrellas came out of the stands, out of the halls, out of the hands, and they flew up the hill, furled and patient, a whole orchard of them, and the town stood there, in the park, in the bunting, under a sky that was suddenly full of umbrellas, and the town said, "My umbrellas!" And the town did not know. And the town was about to find out.

And the Keeper pulled, and the Keeper pulled, and the Keeper pulled, and the hum was loud, and the hum was proud, and the hum was the hum of a machine that had learned to want, and the machine wanted everything, and the machine was going to keep everything, and the machine did not know when to stop.

"It's out of control," said Barnaby, and he was standing in the Bureau, in the emptying shelves, in the clean squares, and his whiskers were down, and his voice was the voice of a walrus who has been the Chief for a very long time and has just seen a thing he was afraid of. "It's out of control. The missing gear. The growing. The... the letting the captain go. It compensated. It kept harder. And now it's... now it's keeping everything. It's not just keeping the lost things. It's keeping everything. It's... it's learned to want. And it wants everything. And it does not know when to stop."

"Mr. Snell," said Pip. "Mr. Snell has to stop it. Mr. Snell built it. Mr. Snell has to stop it."

"Mr. Snell can't," said Goldie, and she was looking up the hill, at the house, at the new room, at the curtained windows, and her face was the face of a girl who knows every shortcut and has just seen a thing that does not have a shortcut. "Mr. Snell can't stop it. Look." And she pointed. And Pip looked. And Snell was at the gate, at the gate of the house with too many curtains, and he was shaped like an umbrella, and he was waving his long arms, and he was shouting at the house, at the Keeper, at the machine, and the machine did not listen. The machine did not listen. The machine pulled, and the machine pulled, and the machine pulled, and Snell was at the gate, and Snell was waving his long arms, and Snell was shouting, and the machine did not listen.

"He can't stop it," said Pip. "He built it, and he can't stop it. The machine... the machine learned to want. And the machine wants everything. And the machine does not know when to stop." He looked at the Bureau, at the walrus and the cat and the hedgehog and the girl, and he said, "Then we stop it. We stop it. We answer the heartbreak. We show it the love stays. We... we make it stop. We have to. We have to make it stop."

And the Keeper pulled, and the Keeper pulled, and the Keeper pulled, and the town stood there, in the park, in the bunting, with its coats unbuttoned and its socks off and its umbrellas gone, and the town said, "What's happening?" And the town did not know. And the town was about to find out.

But Pip knew. Pip knew. Pip knew that the machine had learned to want, and that the machine wanted everything, and that the machine did not know when to stop, and that the only thing that could stop a machine that keeps was a thing that lets go. And the thing that lets go was the love stays. And the love stays was the answer. And the answer was the picnic. And the picnic was the bring-a-thing-you-love. And the bring-a-thing-you-love was the thing they were going to show the machine.

"Get the pocket," said Pip. "Get the pocket. Get the marble. Get the paperclip. Get the rubber band. Get the button. Get the label. Get the socks. Get the lonely socks. Get everything. We're going up the hill. We're going to answer the heartbreak. We're going to make the machine stop."

And Goldie patted the pocket, and the marble and the paperclip and the rubber band clicked together, the small, satisfied jingle, the jingle of three useless things that were ready, that had been waiting for a job, that were going to help. And Thimble's quills clicked, and the buttons were there, the spare buttons, the promises, the *I expect you to come loose, and I expect to fix you, and I am not going anywhere.* And Miss Whisk stamped the floor, DO NOT, and the stamp was the truth, and the truth was no. And Barnaby leaked, and the leaking was the leaking of a walrus who was going to say no, and the no was going to be a very large, very kind no.

And they went to the Bureau, to the emptying shelves, to the clean squares, to plan. Because you did not go up the hill to answer a heartbreak without a plan. Because the plan was the thing. Because the plan was the pocket and the button and the socks and the label, and the plan was the how, and the how was the thing they needed.

And the town watched them go. And the town said, "Who are they?" And the town did not know. And the town was about to find out.

But that was later. That was the climax. That was the answer.

For now, there was a machine that had learned to want, and a town with its buttons off, and a pocket full of ready things, and a boy who had decided to make a machine stop.

For now, that was enough.
