## Chapter 26 — The Heart of the House

They went to arrange the dinosaur, and Snell showed them the Keeper, and the Keeper was the heart of the house, and the heart of the house was a machine that was too big for the house.

"Come," said Snell, when they had finished their tea, when the biscuits, the dog kind, had been eaten, because you did not leave biscuits unfinished in a house that had made them. "Come. You should see it. You should see the Keeper. If… if you're going to understand, you should see it. I've never shown it to anyone. Nobody's seen it. But you… you've seen the whole thing. You've seen the almost. And I think… I think you should see the heart."

He led them down. Down a stair that had not been there, down into a room that the outside of the house did not have room for, down into the heart of the house. And the Keeper was there.

The Keeper was brass. It was brass and gears and springs and little copper wires, and it was the size of a room, and it was growing. Pip could see it growing. It was a slow thing, a thing you saw the way you see a plant grow, which is to say you saw it if you looked, and if you looked away and looked back it was a little bigger. The gears turned. The springs coiled and uncoiled. The copper wires hummed. And the whole thing was warm and cold at the same time, warm like a thing that is loved, cold like a thing that is kept, and it hummed, patient and keeping, and the hum was the hum they had been hearing all week, the hum under the floor, the hum under the hill, and it was loud here, and it was the loudest it had ever been, and it was a hum you felt in your teeth, in your bones, in the part of you that knows when a thing is too big for the room it is in.

"Is it alive?" Ollie asked, very quietly, for him.

Snell looked at the machine, and his face did the thing, the soft-sad thing. "No," he said. "No, it's not alive. It's… it's a machine. It's a machine that keeps. It's not alive." He paused, and his long hand rested on the brass, and the Keeper hummed happy. "But sometimes… sometimes I think it's the closest thing to alive I've got. And that's… that's the sad part. That's the sad part, isn't it. That the closest thing to alive I've got is a machine that keeps."

And Ollie, who was six, who was brave in small, loud ways, said, "That's the saddest thing I ever heard," and Snell said, "Yes. Yes, it is," and the Keeper hummed, and nobody said anything for a moment.

"This is the Keeper," said Snell, and his voice was proud and afraid, both at once. "This is… this is the thing I built. So nothing I love is ever lost again. It keeps. It keeps everything. It brings the lost things here, and it keeps them, and they're never lost again." He put his long hand on the brass, gently, the way he did everything, and the Keeper hummed happy, the way it did when he was close. "It hums happy when I'm close. It's the only thing that hums happy when I'm close."

And Pip, who was eleven, who had a label maker, who was looking at the heart of the house, saw the thing that made his stomach do a thing. In the middle of the Keeper, in the very heart of it, there was a jar. A small glass jar, set into the brass, held there by the gears, the way a heart is held by the ribs. And in the jar there was something small. Something metal. Something that caught the lamplight.

"What's in the jar?" Pip asked.

Snell went very still. His long hand folded tighter. And for a long moment he did not speak. And then he said, in a voice that was so soft it was almost not there, "The first thing. The first thing I kept. The… the thing that started it." He did not say what it was. He did not have to. Pip looked at the small metal thing in the jar, and he thought: *a collar tag. A dog's collar tag. Biscuit's collar tag.* And he filed it, and he put a little star on the tag, and he did not say it out loud, because the man was not ready, and because you did not shout at a bell, you rang it softly.

"The Keeper keeps the first thing in the heart," said Snell. "So it remembers. So it remembers what it's for. So nothing it loves is ever lost again." He looked at the jar, at the small metal thing, and his face was the face of a man looking at a thing he has lost and has not stopped loving. "So it remembers the dog. So it remembers… so it remembers Biscuit."

And Pip, who was eleven, who had a label maker, who had just seen the heart of the house, felt the shape of the answer settle into him, hard and clear. The Keeper was built around a dog's collar tag. The Keeper was a love letter to a dog, written in brass and gears, and the love letter was eating the town, and the love letter was going to keep everything on Finding Day, and the love letter did not know that the love stays, even when the dog is gone.

"Mr. Snell," said Pip. "The Keeper. It's… it's too big for the house. It's growing. And on Finding Day, it's going to keep everything. Everything that's lost. Everything that's loved. And the people are going to forget. And the things are going to be cold. And the almost is going to be… the almost is going to be everything. Please. Please don't. Please let them go home. Please let the love stay."

And Snell, who was a man shaped like an umbrella, who had been alone eight years, looked at the Keeper, at the heart of the house, at the jar with the small metal thing, and the crack was there, wide, wider than it had ever been, and for a long moment Pip thought the man was going to say yes. And then the man said, "Watch me."

"Watch me," said Silas Snell, the saddest man in town. "Watch me. I'm going to keep everything. And I'm going to prove it. I'm going to prove that you can keep everything you love. Watch me." He paused, and his voice went soft. "But the dinosaur. The dinosaur I'll let go home. The dinosaur I'll let go home. Because the boy has a dinosaur-shaped hole. And I know the hole. And I'll let the dinosaur go home. Today. Before Finding Day. Before… before the Keeper keeps everything. I'll let the dinosaur go home first. That's the deal. That's the deal."

And that was the deal. One dinosaur, let go home first, before the Keeper kept everything. The rest kept. The rest kept, on Finding Day, when the Keeper was going to keep everything, and nothing would ever be lost again, and nothing would ever be warm again.

"Thank you," said Pip. "For the dinosaur. Thank you. But we're not done. We're not done, Mr. Snell. We're going to show you. We're going to show you the love stays. And we're going to do it on Finding Day. And you're going to see."

"Watch me," said Snell, softly, to himself, the way you say a thing you are trying to believe.

And they went up the stair that had not been there, up out of the heart of the house, up into the keeping room, and the Keeper hummed behind them, patient and keeping, growing, getting ready for Finding Day. And Pip, at the door, looked back, and he saw the jar in the heart of the machine, the small metal thing, the first thing, the thing that started it, and he thought: *the love stays. Even when the dog is gone. The love stays. And the Keeper is built around the love. And the Keeper does not know that the love stays without the keeping. And we're going to prove it.*

They went out into the lane, into the dusk, and the house with too many curtains held its breath, and the Keeper hummed, patient and keeping, and tomorrow was Finding Day, and the Keeper was going to keep everything, and one dinosaur was going to go home today.

But tonight, there was a heart of brass, and a jar with a small metal thing, and a man saying *watch me* to himself, softly, the way you say a thing you are trying to believe.

And tonight, that was a start.
