## Chapter 8 — The Cat Who Kept the Record

Miss Whisk did not like visitors, and she did not like interruptions, and she did not like the way Barnaby leaked on the intake log, and she made all three of these things clear by the simple method of continuing to work while Pip and Ollie stood in front of her desk, waiting.

Her desk was the tidiest thing in the Bureau. Everything on it had a place, and everything was in its place, and the places were labeled in a handwriting so precise it looked printed. IN. OUT. PENDING. DO NOT. There was a stamp for each, and Miss Whisk used them the way a pianist uses keys, without looking, with a kind of furious grace.

"What's the DO NOT stamp for?" Ollie asked, pointing.

"Things that must not," said Miss Whisk.

"Like what?"

"Like being touched by six-year-olds," said Miss Whisk, and stamped the air near Ollie's hand, DO NOT, and Ollie giggled, which was the first giggle anyone had gotten out of her all week, and she pretended it had not happened, which was also a kind of grace.

"You're the dinosaur people," she said, not looking up.

"Captain Chomp," said Ollie. "He's a secret pirate."

"He's a dinosaur who was lost and is now found and is being loved back, which is more than most things get, so he should count himself lucky." Miss Whisk stamped something IN. "I'm the Head Cataloguer. I keep the record. Every thing that comes in, every thing that goes out. If it's not in the record, it didn't happen. If it is in the record, it happened whether you like it or not. The record is the truth. I am the truth's secretary. It's a living."

Pip, who loved records, who loved labels, who loved the idea that a thing could be written down and therefore held, felt an immediate and embarrassing kinship with this cat. "Can I see it?" he asked. "The record?"

Miss Whisk looked up for the first time. She looked at Pip the way you look at a dog that has offered to do your taxes. "You want to see the record."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because," said Pip, and he thought about it, because it was a real question, "because if it's written down, it can't get lost. The record keeps the things. Even if the things go somewhere, the record still has them. That's… that's the whole point of writing things down. Isn't it?"

Miss Whisk stared at him for a long moment. Then she did something Pip would remember for the rest of his life, which was that the corner of her mouth moved, very slightly, in a direction that was almost a smile.

"Huh," she said. "The boy gets it." She heaved the great ledger around so it faced him. "Fine. Look. Don't touch. The record is not a pet."

The ledger was enormous. It was the size of a door, and it was full of entries, each one in Miss Whisk's precise hand. Pip read them, and they were not what he expected. He had expected a list of objects. What he got was a list of loves.

*One red mitten. Left hand. Knitted by a grandmother, since gone. The wool remembers her hands. Claimed by a girl who cries when she holds it. Status: waiting. Be gentle.*

*One toy car, one wheel. Belonged to a boy who is now taller. The boy does not want the car. The boy wants the being-small. We cannot file the being-small. We keep the car. Status: complicated.*

*One spectacle frame, no lens. Belonged to a man who read to his dog. The dog is gone. The man reads to the frame. Status: waiting. Extra dusting.*

Pip read, and read, and felt his chest do the thing it had been doing all week, the rearranging, the furniture-moving. The record was not a list of things. It was a list of what the things meant. Miss Whisk did not catalogue objects. She catalogued the loving.

"You write down the love," he said.

"I write down the relevant information," said Miss Whisk, but her ears did a small, pleased thing. "The love is relevant. A thing without its love is just inventory. We are not a warehouse. We are a… " She paused, and for a second the precise, sarcastic surface went thin, and underneath it was something so tender it made Pip look away to give her privacy. "We are a waiting room," she said. "For the loving. The things are just the coats it wears."

Ollie, who had been quiet for an unusually long time, said, "Do you have a coat?"

Miss Whisk went very still.

"I beg your pardon," she said.

"A coat," said Ollie, undeterred, six and honest. "For your loving. Do you have a thing you're waiting for?"

The silence that followed had edges. Pip grabbed for Ollie's hand, but Ollie did not need grabbing; he stood there, small and loud and unafraid, asking the question nobody asked the cat, and Miss Whisk looked at him, and her tail went still, and her spectacles caught the lamplight so Pip couldn't see her eyes.

"I have a file," she said, at last. Her voice was flat. "Everyone has a file. Mine is in the back. It is not interesting. It is a bell." She turned the ledger back around, firmly, the audience over. "A bell. From a collar. It is lost. It has been lost a long time. I do not wait for it. I merely keep the file. There is a difference. The file is administrative."

But her paw, Pip noticed, had gone to her own collar — a plain leather collar with no bell on it — and touched it, once, the way you touch a place where something used to be. And then she stamped something PENDING with more force than was necessary, and said, "Go love your dinosaur. The record keeps itself. That's the point of the record."

Pip went. He pulled Ollie along. But at the end of the aisle he looked back, and Miss Whisk was not stamping. She was looking at the ledger, at one particular entry, and her paw was on her empty collar, and her face was the face of someone who kept the record of everyone's loving and had filed her own in the back, under *administrative*, where nobody would think to look.

Pip filed it. He filed it carefully, with a little star on the tag, the way Thimble did. *Be gentle with this one.*

They found Captain Chomp on his shelf, and Ollie told him about the cat and the bell and the record, and Captain Chomp's loose button caught the light. And it was then, as Pip reached to straighten the dinosaur's tag, that he saw the thing that would keep him awake that night.

On the shelf next to Captain Chomp, there was a tag. A neat little tag, in Miss Whisk's precise hand. It said:

*One music box. Plays a waltz. Belonged to a woman who danced in her kitchen. Claimed. Ready. Status: going home on Finding Day.*

But there was no music box.

There was a clean square in the dust, the exact shape of a music box, and a tag that said it was there, and nothing else. Pip looked at the clean square, and at the tag, and at the shelf, and he felt the floor tilt the way it had tilted in Ollie's room.

"Ollie," he said. "Where's the music box?"

Ollie looked. "There isn't one."

"The tag says there is."

"Then the tag's wrong," said Ollie, with the easy logic of six.

But Pip knew, with a certainty that went from his stomach to his shoes, that the tag was not wrong. Miss Whisk's tags were never wrong. The record was the truth. And the truth said there was a music box here, a music box that played a waltz, that belonged to a woman who danced in her kitchen, that was claimed and ready and going home on Finding Day.

And the truth was a clean square in the dust.

Pip looked down the long, quiet aisle, at the shelves and the bins and the waiting things, and for the first time the Bureau did not feel like a waiting room. It felt like a room where something had been, and was not, and had not left a note.

From somewhere far off, very faintly, came a humming.
