## Chapter 19 - What the Baker Knew

On the fifth day, before they went to knock on the door of the man shaped like an umbrella, Pip and Goldie went to The Warm Loaf, because Mrs. Crumb knew things, and because you did not go to answer a heartbreak without asking the baker first.

Mrs. Crumb was kneading. She kneaded the way Barnaby leaked, constantly and without apology, and she listened to the whole thing - the hum, the buttons, the music box, the man shaped like an umbrella who caught things gently - and she did not interrupt, and she did not say *I told you so*, and when they were done she wiped her hands on her apron and said, "Sit down. You're going to want a bun for this."

They sat. She gave them buns. And then she told them what she knew.

"Silas Snell," she said, "has been buying brass for eight years."

"Brass?" said Goldie.

"Brass. And gears. And springs. And little copper wires. And things I don't have names for." Mrs. Crumb leaned on the counter, and her floury forearms were crossed, and her face was the face of a woman who has been watching a slow sadness for a long time and has decided to finally say so. "He used to come in for the loaf, every morning, with the dog. And then the dog ran off, and he stopped coming in for the loaf. But he started coming in for the brass. He'd order it through the hardware, and the hardware boy would bring it up the hill, and I'd see the boxes. Brass. Gears. Springs. Week after week. Month after month. Eight years." She shook her head. "You don't buy brass for eight years unless you're building something. And you don't build something for eight years unless you're trying to fix something that can't be fixed."

"The Keeper," said Pip.

"The Keeper," Mrs. Crumb agreed. "That's what he calls it. I heard him say it once, to the hardware boy. *It's for the Keeper,* he said. *So nothing I love is ever lost again.*" She looked at Pip. "The hardware boy thought it was a cupboard. I knew it wasn't a cupboard. You don't say *so nothing I love is ever lost again* about a cupboard. You say it about a... " She paused. "You say it about a promise you're making to a ghost."

The silence that followed had a temperature, and the temperature was eight years of brass.

"Has it been growing?" Pip asked. "The machine?"

Mrs. Crumb nodded, slowly. "I think so. I can't see it. The curtains are closed. But I can hear it. The hum. It used to be a little hum, a quiet hum, a hum you'd miss if you weren't listening. And now it's a big hum. A hum you feel in your teeth. And the hill..." She paused, and she looked out the window, toward the curtained house. "The hill sounds different. The hill sounds full. Like there's more in it than there used to be. Like the house is... holding more than a house should hold." She looked at Pip. "I think the machine is growing. I think it's been growing for eight years. And I think it's getting to the point where it's too big for the house. And I think, when a machine that keeps things gets too big for the house, it starts keeping the house. And then it starts keeping the hill. And then..." She did not finish. She did not have to.

"And then it starts keeping the town," said Pip.

Mrs. Crumb looked at him, and her face was the face of a woman who has been afraid of a thing for a long time and has just heard someone else say it out loud, and the saying-it-out-loud is a relief and a terror at the same time.

"Yes," she said. "And then it starts keeping the town. And I've been leaving the bowl out, and I've been making the loaf, and I've been hoping, because hoping is easier than knowing. And now you've come, and you know, and you're going to knock on his door." She looked at Pip with her kind, floury face. "So I'm going to tell you the thing I know about Silas Snell, the thing that matters, the thing you need to know before you knock."

"What is it?" said Pip.

"He's not going to yell at you," said Mrs. Crumb. "He's not going to be mean. He's going to offer you tea. He's going to be gentle. He's going to be so gentle it breaks your heart. Because he's not a man who hates. He's a man who loves too much and doesn't know where to put it. And when you knock, he's going to open the door, and he's going to be glad to see you, because he's been alone eight years, and a knock on the door is the best thing that's happened to him in eight years." She paused, and her voice went soft. "And that's the hard part. Because it's easy to be brave with a man who yells. It's hard to be brave with a man who offers you tea. You have to be brave enough to tell him the truth, even when he's gentle. Even when he's glad to see you. Even when it breaks his heart. Because the truth is the only thing that's going to save him. And the truth is going to hurt."

Pip felt the ground under the fog go solid and sad, because Mrs. Crumb had just told him the hardest thing about answering a heartbreak, which was that the heartbreak was going to be kind, and the kindness was going to make it harder, and he was going to have to be brave enough to tell the truth to a man who offered him tea.

"I'll be brave," he said.

"I know you will," said Mrs. Crumb. "You've got the look. You've got the label-maker look. The look of a boy who's going to put the world back in order." She reached under the counter, and she brought out a loaf, a warm one, wrapped in a cloth. "Take this. For him. Nobody should have to face a hard conversation on an empty stomach. And nobody should have to answer a heartbreak without a loaf. It's a rule. I just made it up. But it's true."

Pip took the loaf. It was warm. It smelled like the inside of a good decision. And he thought of the bowl by the door, the blue bowl with the chipped rim, full of clean water, and he thought of Mrs. Crumb, who kept the bowl out for a stray she did not own, and who made the loaf for a man who had stopped coming in, and who had been leaving the bowl out and making the loaf for eight years, because leaving the bowl out and making the loaf was the only thing left that looked like love.

"Mrs. Crumb," he said. "The stray. The one who drinks from the bowl. Have you ever seen it?"

Mrs. Crumb looked at him, and her face did the soft thing. "Now and then. Early morning. A biscuit-colored dog, fat as a loaf, happy as you please. It drinks, and it warms itself by the oven, and it goes before the shop's busy." She paused, and she looked out the window, toward the hill, and something moved in her face, a thought, a almost-thought, a thing she almost said and then did not. "It's a stray. It sleeps where it likes. I don't ask it where. That's the rule. You leave the bowl out. You don't ask the stray to explain itself."

Pip nodded. He did not know why his heart was beating a little faster. He filed the almost-thought, the thing Mrs. Crumb had almost said, with a little star on the tag. And he thought: *the dog is not the love. The dog is the thing the love wears. And the love is still there. Even after eight years. Even with the curtains closed.* He did not know where the stray slept. He did not know it was close. He only knew that the bowl was full, and that somebody drank from it, and that leaving it out was a kind of love that did not need to own the thing it loved.

"Thank you for the loaf," he said.

"Thank you for knocking," said Mrs. Crumb. "Eight years is a long time to leave a bowl out. It's nice to think somebody's finally going to ring the bell."

And Pip and Goldie went out into the town, into the morning, toward the hill, toward the house with too many curtains, carrying a warm loaf and a brass button and the shape of the answer, and behind them Mrs. Crumb refilled the bowl, the way she did every morning, the way she had every morning for eight years, and under the proofing rack, where it was warm, where the bread rose, a biscuit-colored dog slept like it had never had a worry in its life.

But that was later. That was Finding Day. That was the reunion.

For now, there was a door to knock on, and a man shaped like an umbrella, and a loaf, and a truth that was going to hurt.

For now, that was enough.
