## Chapter 25 — The Bureau Declares a Working Holiday

On the seventh day, the Bureau declared a working holiday, which was a thing Barnaby made up, and which meant that everyone worked, but with biscuits, and with a sense of occasion, and with the particular grim cheer of people who are preparing for the best day of the year while knowing that the best day of the year might be ruined.

"Finding Day," Barnaby announced, standing on a lost step, leaking contentedly, "is the best day. The best day of the year. The day the big door opens. The day the way home is straight. The day everything that is ready goes home." He paused, and his whiskers moved, and he said, "This year, the Keeper is going to try to keep everything. This year, the best day might be… might be the worst day. So we are going to prepare. We are going to prepare with biscuits. And with a sense of occasion. And with the grim cheer of people who are not going to let the best day be the worst day. I have made up a title for it. You are all now Knights of the Grim Cheer. It is not official. But it is true."

"I'm already a Knight of the Shortcut," said Goldie. "Can I be two knights?"

"You can be two knights," said Barnaby. "You can be all the knights. The knighthood is not a limited resource. The knighthood is a feeling."

And so they prepared. They prepared the way the Bureau prepared, which was with dusting, and with labeling, and with the particular furious grace of people who believe that writing a thing down is a way of keeping it. Miss Whisk went through the ledger, and she marked the things that were ready, the things that were claimed, the things that were going home, and she did not mark the eighteen clean squares, because the eighteen clean squares were not going home, and the marking of them would have been a lie, and Miss Whisk did not lie, because the record was the truth.

But she did something else. She took a fresh page, and she wrote, at the top, in her precise hand, THE EIGHTEEN, and under it she wrote the names. The music box. The telescope. The harmonica. The red mitten. And the rest. She wrote them all, the eighteen, and under each one she wrote the person who loved it, the person who was waiting, the person who was going to forget if the thing stayed lost too long. And she set the page where everyone could see it, and she said, "We count them. We remember them. And we bring them home. That's the job. That's the whole job."

Thimble prepared the socks. He went through the sock wing, and he sorted the lonely socks, the single socks, the ones that smelled like toast and rain and grass and someone else's house, and he put them in a special bin, a bin he labeled READY / GOING HOME / SMELLS LIKE SOMEONE. And Pip, watching, said, "Why those? Why the lonely ones?"

"Because the lonely ones want their pairs," said Thimble. "A lonely sock wants its pair more than anything. More than a glove wants its glove. More than a key wants its lock. A lonely sock is a homing thing. It wants home. It wants home more than anything." He placed a small gray sock in the bin, gently. "On Finding Day, the lonely socks go home. They find their pairs. They find their people. They… they lead the way. The lonely socks lead the way home. That's the thing about lonely socks. They're not just lost. They're… they're homing. They're homing things."

And Pip, who was eleven, who had a label maker, who had just heard a hedgehog say *the lonely socks lead the way home*, felt something click, quietly, in the drawer where he kept the things that mattered. He filed it. He filed it with a little star on the tag. *The lonely socks lead the way home.* He did not know why it mattered. He did not know it was going to matter more than anything. He only knew that it was a thing worth filing, and he filed it, and he went back to the dusting.

Barnaby prepared the big door. He went to the back of the Bureau, to the great door that none of them had ever seen open, the one that Snell had come through, the one that was the only way in or out, and he polished the brass, and he oiled the hinges, and he said, "Tomorrow, this door opens. Tomorrow, the way home is straight. Tomorrow, everything that is ready goes home." He paused, and his flipper rested on the brass, and he said, "And tomorrow, the Keeper is going to try to keep everything. And tomorrow, we are going to answer the heartbreak. And tomorrow, we are going to prove that the love stays." He looked at Pip with his small, kind eyes. "Are you ready, Friend of the Lost?"

"I'm ready," said Pip. And he was. He was ready. He had a pocket full of useless junk that was going to help, and a cat with a stamp, and a walrus who says no, and a hedgehog who sorts the socks, and a six-year-old who counts to zero. He was ready. He did not know the how. He did not know the exactly. But he was ready, and being ready was the thing you could do, and the how was the thing that would come, and the how would come because he was ready.

Ollie prepared the counting. He took his page, the one that said OFFICIAL COUNTER, OLLIE PARKER, AGE 6, VERY GOOD AT COUNTING, and he wrote the number at the top. NINETEEN. And under it he wrote, GOING TO ZERO. And he said, "I'm going to count to zero. I'm going to count the things home. And when I get to zero, that means nothing's missing. And zero is the best number. And I'm going to get to zero." He looked at Pip with his whole face. "I promise. That's my job. I'm the Official Counter. I count to zero."

And Pip, who was eleven, who had a brother who was very good at counting, felt the ground under the fog go solid, because a room where a six-year-old promises to count to zero is a room that has decided what winning looks like. Zero. Nothing missing. Everything home.

They prepared until the lamps were low, and the shadows were long, and the lost things stood in their rows like a crowd that had been told to wait and had decided to be patient about it. And the umbrella orchard made its rain-that-wasn't-there sound, and the jar of keys hummed its question, and under the floor, under the hill, the Keeper hummed, patient and keeping, getting ready for Finding Day.

And Barnaby, at the end, when the biscuits were gone and the dusting was done, said, "Tomorrow is Finding Day. Tomorrow is the best day. And tomorrow, we are going to answer the heartbreak. And I want you to remember something. I want you to remember that the man in the curtained house is not a monster. He is a bell. And we do not shout at a bell. We ring it. We ring it softly, and we listen, and we hope it remembers the sound." He looked at Pip. "And I want you to remember that the love stays. Even when the thing is lost. Even when the thing is kept. Even when the thing is gone. The love stays. That's the thing we're going to prove. That's the whole thing. The love stays."

"The love stays," said Pip.

"The love stays," said Goldie.

"The love stays," said Ollie, at full volume, which was his way of signing a contract.

And Miss Whisk, who was the Head Cataloguer, who kept the record of everyone's loving, who had a bell in the back under administrative, said, "The love stays," and her paw went to her empty collar, and she left the drawer open a little, so it got some air, so it knew it was not forgotten.

And Thimble, who sorted the socks, who gave spare buttons to lost coats, who was prepared for loss, said, "The love stays," and his quills clicked, and the red star button was still gone, and he was going to get it back, and he was going to have a word.

And under the hill, the Keeper hummed, patient and keeping, and it had nineteen things, and it wanted the twentieth, and it was getting bigger, and it was getting ready.

But the Bureau was ready too. The Bureau was ready with biscuits, and with a sense of occasion, and with the grim cheer of people who are not going to let the best day be the worst day.

And tomorrow was Finding Day. And tomorrow, they were going to prove that the love stays.

But tonight, there was a working holiday, and a page that said THE EIGHTEEN, and a bin of lonely socks that smelled like someone, and a six-year-old who was going to count to zero.

And tonight, that was a start.
