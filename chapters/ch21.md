## Chapter 21 - The House That Held Its Breath

They were halfway down the lane when Snell called them back.

"Wait," he said. He was standing in the doorway, and he was shaped like an umbrella, and his long hands were folded, and his face was the face of a man who has just done a thing he cannot take back. "Wait. You haven't seen the whole thing. Nobody's seen the whole thing. I... " He paused, and the gladness was there again, the gladness of a man who has been alone eight years and cannot bear for his visitors to leave. "Would you like to see the whole thing? I could show you. I could show you everything. I've been... I've been keeping it all. And nobody's seen it. And I think... I think I would like someone to see it."

And Pip, who had come to confront a thief, who had a brass button in his pocket and the shape of the answer in his head, felt the ground under the fog go warm and sad at the same time, because the man was not asking them to see a machine. The man was asking them to see *him*. The man was saying *I have been keeping all this love, and nobody has seen it, and I would like someone to see it.* And that was the hardest thing to say no to in the world.

"Yes," said Pip. "We'd like to see it."

And Snell smiled, the real one, the one that changed his whole face, and he ushered them back in, and he said, "Tea first. You can't see the whole thing without tea. It's a rule. I just made it up. But it's true."

They had tea. And then Snell showed them the house.

The house was bigger than it had been.

Pip noticed it slowly, the way you notice a thing that is too big to notice all at once. The main room, the one with the shelves and the music box, was the same. But beyond it there was another room. And beyond that, another. And beyond that, another. Rooms Pip was sure had not been there, rooms that the outside of the house did not have room for, rooms that went back and back, like the Bureau, like the Bureau exactly, and Pip felt the two rooms click together in his head, the Bureau and the house, both of them full of kept things, both of them going back and back, and he thought: *the house is a Bureau. The house is a Bureau that keeps instead of waits.*

"This is the keeping room," said Snell, and he showed them a room of single gloves, each one dusted, each one labeled. "And this is the music room." A room of music boxes, none of them playing. "And this is the reading room." A room of books, each one closed, each one dusted. "And this is the... " He paused, and he opened a door, and behind the door was a room that was full of umbrellas, furled and patient, a whole orchard of them, and Pip thought of the Bureau's umbrella orchard, and he thought: *he's copying it. He's copying the Bureau. He's building a Bureau that keeps.*

"Mr. Snell," said Pip. "These rooms. Were they always here?"

Snell was quiet for a moment. "No," he said. "They grew."

"They grew?"

"The Keeper grows," said Snell, and he said it the way you say a thing you are proud of and a thing you are afraid of, both at once. "The Keeper keeps. And the more it keeps, the bigger it gets. And the bigger it gets, the more room it needs. And the house... the house makes room." He looked at the rooms, at the kept things, and his face was the face of a man looking at a thing he has built and cannot control. "The house makes room. I don't know how. I don't ask. The house makes room, and the Keeper fills it, and I dust it, and I love it, and it is never lost." He paused, and his voice went soft. "It's a lot of room. It's more room than there used to be. Sometimes I... sometimes I get lost in it. Sometimes I go into a room and I don't remember which room it is. And I have to call out, and the house... the house answers. The house shows me the way out." He looked at Pip. "The house is very kind. The house is very kind to me."

And Pip, who was eleven, who had a label maker, who had just walked through a house that grew rooms, felt the ground under the fog go cold, because a house that grows rooms is a house that is being eaten, and a man who gets lost in his own house is a man who is being kept, and the Keeper was not just keeping the things. The Keeper was keeping the house. And the Keeper was keeping the man.

"Mr. Snell," said Pip, carefully. "Do you ever go outside?"

Snell was quiet for a long moment. "Sometimes," he said. "To the gate. To get the brass. The hardware boy brings the brass." He paused. "I don't go much further than the gate. The Keeper... the Keeper likes me to be here. The Keeper likes me to be close. It hums when I'm close. It hums... it hums happy when I'm close." He looked at Pip with his kind, tired eyes. "It's the only thing that hums happy when I'm close. So I stay close."

The silence that followed had a temperature, and the temperature was a man who stayed close to a machine because it was the only thing that hummed happy when he was close.

Goldie, who had been quiet, who had been looking at the kept things with her shortcut eyes, said, "Mr. Snell. Can I ask you a thing?"

"Anything," said Snell, who was glad to be asked anything, who was glad to be asked at all.

"These things," said Goldie. "The gloves. The music boxes. The books. Do you... do you use them? Do you play the music boxes? Do you read the books? Do you wear the gloves?"

Snell was quiet for a long moment. "No," he said. "No, I don't use them. I keep them. Using them... using them would wear them out. Using them would... would risk them. I keep them safe. I keep them dusted. I keep them... I keep them." He looked at the shelves, at the kept things, and his face was the face of a man who has just heard himself say the thing and has not yet understood it. "I keep them. I don't use them. I keep them so they're never lost. And I... " He paused, and the crack opened, just a little, just a crack. "And I don't use them. I don't play the music boxes. I don't read the books. I just... keep them."

"Because you're afraid," said Goldie, gently, "that if you use them, you'll lose them."

"Yes," said Snell, and the word came out so soft it was almost not there. "Yes. That's it. That's the whole thing. If I use them, I'll lose them. If I play the music box, the song will end. If I read the book, the book will be finished. If I wear the gloves, the gloves will wear out. And I can't... I can't lose anything else. I can't. So I keep them. I keep them unused. I keep them... I keep them almost." He looked at Goldie with his kind, tired eyes. "I keep them almost. That's the whole thing. I keep them almost."

And Pip, who was eleven, who had a label maker, who had just heard a man say *I keep them almost*, felt the shape of the answer settle into him, hard and clear, like a label pressed onto a thing that was not going anywhere. The man was keeping everything almost. Almost played. Almost read. Almost worn. Almost loved. Because almost was safe, and almost was never lost, and almost was a cage, and the man did not know it was a cage, and the man was being kept in it too.

"Mr. Snell," said Pip. "Can we come back tomorrow? We have... we have a thing to show you. A thing about the keeping. A thing about the almost."

Snell looked at him, and the gladness was there, the plain, hungry gladness. "Tomorrow," he said. "Yes. Come back tomorrow. I'll make tea. I'll make... I'll make biscuits. The dog kind. I always make the dog kind. I don't know why. I always make the dog kind." He paused, and his face did the thing, the soft-sad thing, the thing that was a dog-shaped hole. "I always make the dog kind. And there's no dog. And I make them anyway. I make them anyway."

Pip felt the ground under the fog go warm and sad. The man was leaving the bowl out. The man was making the dog kind. The man was leaving the bowl out, in his way, in the only way he knew how, and the bowl was a machine, and the machine was eating the town, and the man did not know, and the man was kind, and the man was heartbroken, and the man was going to make biscuits tomorrow, the dog kind, and there was no dog, and he was going to make them anyway.

"We'll come back," said Pip. "Tomorrow. We'll bring... we'll bring a thing. A thing about the almost."

And they went out into the lane, into the dusk, and the house with too many curtains held its breath behind them, and the Keeper hummed, patient and keeping, and the house had grown rooms, and the man was being kept, and the biscuits were going to be the dog kind.

Tonight there was a house that grew rooms, and a man who kept everything almost, and a boy who had decided to show him the difference between almost and home.
