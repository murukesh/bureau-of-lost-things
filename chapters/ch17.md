## Chapter 17 — Goldie Minds (Just This Once)

The Keeper came for Goldie's pocket on the fifth day, at eleven in the morning, which was rude, because eleven was bun o'clock, and Barnaby was low, and nobody should have to deal with a machine that keeps things on an empty stomach.

It started with the hum. The hum, which was always under the floor, under the hill, came up through the Bureau like a tide, and it was louder than it had ever been, and it was not careful this time, it was not turned down, it was reaching, and the socks in the sock wing leaned away from it, all of them, a whole field of small gray wheat in a wind that came from below.

"It's reaching," said Thimble, looking up from the rain ones. "It's reaching further. The missing gear. It's compensating. I told you. A machine that's missing a gear pulls harder."

And then Goldie's pocket jingled.

It jingled the way it always jingled, the small, satisfied jingle of the marble and the paperclip and the rubber band. But this time the jingle was wrong. This time the jingle was a jingle that was moving, and Goldie looked down, and her face did a thing Pip had never seen it do, which was afraid.

The pocket was pulling. The bulge of it was sliding, toward the seam, toward the hole she'd never fixed, and the marble was rolling, and the paperclip was sliding, and the rubber band was stretching, and all three of them were going, going, toward the hole, toward the floor, toward the hum.

"No," said Goldie.

She grabbed the pocket. She grabbed it with both hands, and she held it shut, and the pull went on, and it was strong, stronger than a pocket should have to fight, and Goldie's heels skidded on the floor, and she said, "No, no, no, you don't, you do not, those are mine—"

"Goldie!" Pip grabbed her arm. Ollie grabbed her other arm, which was not helpful, because Ollie was six and weighed about as much as a determined cat, but he grabbed it anyway, because he was brave in small, loud ways. Barnaby heaved himself up from the desk and put his great gray bulk between Goldie and the floor, between Goldie and the hum, and he said, in his deep, wet, certain voice, "No. You do not. Not in my Bureau. Not while I am the Chief."

And Miss Whisk did the thing that saved them, which was that she stamped the floor.

She stamped it with the DO NOT stamp, the one she used for things that must not, and she stamped it right on the floor where the pull was strongest, and she said, "This is a Bureau. This is a waiting room. This is not a keeping room. And you—" she stamped again, DO NOT, DO NOT, "—are not allowed to keep here. The record says so. And I am the truth's secretary. And the truth says NO."

And the pull stopped.

It did not fade. It stopped, all at once, the way a radio stops when someone unplugs it, and the silence that replaced it was the silence of a machine that has been told no by a cat with a stamp, and the marble rolled back into the pocket, and the paperclip slid back, and the rubber band snapped back into shape, and Goldie's pocket jingled the small, satisfied jingle, and Goldie sat down on the floor, hard, and held her pocket with both hands, and breathed.

The silence went on. And then, from under the hill, very faintly, came a sound that was almost a whine. A small, mechanical whine. The sound of a machine that had wanted something and had been told no, and was thinking about it.

"It's thinking," said Goldie, breathing hard. "Pip. It's thinking about it. It's not going to stop. It's just… thinking about how to try again."

"It won't try again here," said Miss Whisk, and she stamped the floor one more time, DO NOT, for good measure. "The record is the truth. And the truth is that this is a waiting room, and waiting rooms do not keep. It can reach. It can pull. But it cannot take, not while the record says no. Not while I am the secretary." She looked at her stamp, and her paw was shaking, just a little, and she put it behind her back so nobody would see. "But it reached. It reached into the Bureau. It has never reached into the Bureau before. The missing gear. It is getting stronger."

Goldie sat on the floor, and she held her pocket, and she did not let go, and Pip sat down next to her, and Ollie sat down on her other side, and they sat there, the three of them, in the silence that had a temperature.

"I minds," said Goldie, at last. Her voice was small. "I know I said I don't mind. I know I said things go, that's what things do. And I meant it. I mean it. When the acorn went, I didn't mind. When the good acorn went, I shrugged." She held the pocket tighter. "But this. This I mind. This I mind a lot."

"Because it wasn't going," said Pip. "It was being taken."

Goldie looked at him. "Yes," she said. "Yes. That's it. That's the whole thing. When a thing goes, that's… that's the thing being a thing. Things go. But when a thing is taken, when a thing is kept, when a thing is put in a cage and told it can't go home — that's not the thing being a thing. That's somebody being afraid. And I don't want my things to be somebody's afraid." She looked at the pocket, at the marble and the paperclip and the rubber band. "These are mine. They're useless. And they're mine. And I love them. And I don't mind when they go. But I mind when they're kept. And I didn't know there was a difference until just now. And there's a difference. There's a whole difference."

Pip felt the ground under the fog go solid, because Goldie had just said the thing he had been trying to say, the thing that was the answer to the heartbreak, the thing that was the crack in the Keeper. The difference between going and being kept. The difference between letting go and being taken. The difference between a bowl left out and a cage locked shut.

"That's it," he said. "Goldie. That's the answer. That's the thing we have to show him. The difference between going and being kept. He thinks he's saving them. But he's not saving them. He's keeping them. And keeping is not saving. Keeping is… keeping is being afraid, with a machine."

Goldie looked at him. And then she grinned, the shortcut grin, the *this is a terrible idea and I'm in* grin, but this time it had something new in it, something hard and clear, something that had been decided.

"Then we show him," she said. "We show him the difference. And we do it with these." She patted the pocket, and the marble and the paperclip and the rubber band clicked together, the small, satisfied jingle. "These three useless things. They're going to help. I can feel it. They've been waiting for a job. That's why I kept them. That's why they're useless. They're useless so they're ready. They're ready for the one job that matters." She looked at Pip. "Don't ask me how I know. I just know. The pocket knows. The pocket's been knowing things for years. I just listen."

And Pip, who was eleven, who had a label maker, who had a friend whose pocket knew things, felt the shape of the answer settle into him, hard and clear, like a label pressed onto a thing that was not going anywhere.

The marble. The paperclip. The rubber band. Three useless things. Three ready things. He did not know how. He did not know when. But he knew, the way Goldie knew, the way the pocket knew, that they were going to matter, that they were going to be the crack in the Keeper, that they were going to help answer the heartbreak.

"Okay," he said. "Okay. We keep them ready. And we keep Captain Chomp safe. And we find the how." He looked at the floor, where Miss Whisk had stamped DO NOT, and at the great door at the back, and at the hill beyond. "The Keeper reached today. It reached for Goldie's pocket. And it got told no. And it's thinking about how to try again." He looked at Barnaby, who was holding Captain Chomp, who was the warmest thing, who was the nineteenth. "It's going to try again. And next time it's going to reach for the warmest thing. So we have to be ready. We have to answer it first."

"Three days," said Barnaby. "Finding Day is in three days."

"Three days," Pip agreed. "And a pocket full of ready things. And a cat with a stamp. And a walrus who says no. And a six-year-old who counts to zero." He looked at Ollie, who was holding his pencil like a sword. "We've got a lot. We've got enough. We just have to find the how."

Under the hill, the Keeper hummed, patient and keeping, thinking about how to try again, and it had eighteen things, and it wanted the nineteenth, and it had just learned that a cat with a stamp could tell it no.

But a machine that keeps things does not like to be told no. And it was going to remember. And it was going to try again.

But that was tomorrow's problem. Tonight there was a pocket full of ready things, and a girl who had learned the difference between going and being kept, and a boy who had decided to answer a heartbreak.

Tonight, that was enough.
