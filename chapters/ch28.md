## Chapter 28 — The Long Way Home

They took the long way home, because Ollie wanted to, because Ollie wanted Captain Chomp to see the town, because Ollie said *he's been lost, he should see everything on the way back, so he remembers it, so he never forgets the way again*, and because Goldie said the long way was actually a shortcut if you thought about it right, which was the kind of thing Goldie said, and which nobody could argue with, because it was Goldie.

So they took the long way. They went down the hill, past the school, past the lost-and-found bin, which was emptier now, because the Bureau had been busy, because the Bureau was always busy, and the red mitten was gone, because the red mitten was kept, because the red mitten was cold, and Pip looked at the empty bin and thought: *tomorrow. Tomorrow the bin gets filled back up. Tomorrow everything goes home. Tomorrow the bin is just a bin again, and not a… not a waiting room.*

They went past the bakery, and The Warm Loaf smelled like the inside of a good decision, and Mrs. Crumb was in the window, hanging a sign, and the sign said FIRST DAY OF FALL PICNIC — TOMORROW — BRING A THING YOU LOVE, and Pip read it, and he thought: *bring a thing you love.* And he thought: *that's the whole thing. That's the whole thing about Finding Day. Bring a thing you love. And let it go home. And let the love stay.*

"Mrs. Crumb!" Ollie called, at full volume, which was his way of saying hello. "Captain Chomp's home! He's home! He's home!"

And Mrs. Crumb came to the door, wiping her floury hands, and she looked at the dinosaur under Ollie's chin, and her face did the soft thing, the kind thing, the thing that had decided a long time ago to be kind and had never found a reason to stop. "Well," she said. "Well, look at that. The captain's home. The secret pirate's home." She looked at Ollie. "I knew he would be. I knew it. I left the bowl out. That's the job. You leave the bowl out, and the things come home." She paused, and she looked at Pip, and something moved in her face, a thought, a almost-thought. "You left the bowl out too, Pip. You and the walrus and the cat. You left the bowl out. And the captain came home." She looked at the hill, toward the curtained house, and her face did the soft-sad thing. "Now if only… if only the man on the hill would leave the bowl out. Now if only he'd let the things come home."

"He will," said Pip. "Tomorrow. He'll see. We're going to show him. The love stays. He'll see."

"I hope so," said Mrs. Crumb. "I've been leaving the bowl out eight years. It's nice to think somebody's finally going to ring the bell." She reached into the window, and she brought out a bun, a warm one, shaped like a knot, and she handed it to Ollie. "For the captain. For the homecoming. It's a rule. I just made it up. But it's true."

And Ollie took the bun, and he fed Captain Chomp a bite of crust, the way he always did, the way he had on the first night in the new house, and Captain Chomp's loose button caught the light, and the homecoming was a homecoming, and the long way home was a long way home, and the town was preparing for the first day of fall.

They went on. They went past the park, where the swing moved by itself in the wind like it was thinking, and past the little library box on the post, and past the bench by the war memorial, and past the gap in the hedge where the cat lived, and the cat looked at them with the flat, unimpressed face of a creature who had never lost anything in its life and did not intend to start, and Goldie greeted the cat by name, because Goldie knew everyone, because Goldie knew every shortcut, and the cat was a shortcut, somehow, the cat was a shortcut to somewhere, and Pip did not ask, because it was Goldie, and because the long way was actually a shortcut if you thought about it right.

And the town was preparing. The town was hanging bunting, and setting out tables, and stringing lights, and the tables were for the picnic, the first-day-of-fall picnic, the bring-a-thing-you-love picnic, and the town was going to sit together, in the park, on the first day of fall, and eat, and be together, and bring a thing they loved. And Pip looked at the tables, at the bunting, at the lights, and he thought: *tomorrow. Tomorrow the town sits together. And tomorrow the things go home. And tomorrow the Keeper keeps everything. And tomorrow we answer the heartbreak.* And he thought: *the picnic is the answer. The picnic is the thing we're going to show him. The picnic is the love stays.*

He did not know how. He did not know the exactly. But he knew the picnic was the answer, the way Goldie knew the pocket knew things, the way the pocket had been knowing things for years, and he filed it, and he put a little star on the tag, and he went on, down the long way, toward the house, toward the home.

And the hum followed.

It was faint, at the edge of things, under the hill, under the town, a low, steady, keeping note, and it followed them the whole long way home, patient and keeping, and it was not careful this time, it was not turned down, it was reaching, and the socks in the Bureau were leaning away from it, and the things in the keeping room were cold, and the Keeper was getting ready for Finding Day. And Pip heard it, and he did not say anything, because Ollie was feeding Captain Chomp a bun, because the homecoming was a homecoming, because the long way home was a long way home, and because the hum was tomorrow's problem.

But Goldie heard it too. And Goldie, who knew every shortcut, who had a pocket full of useless junk, said, quietly, "Pip. The hum. It's following us."

"I know," said Pip.

"It's reaching," said Goldie. "It's reaching for the captain. It let him go, but it's… it's still reaching. It doesn't want to let him go. It's… it's going to try again."

"I know," said Pip. "Tomorrow. Tomorrow we answer it. Tomorrow we show it the love stays. Tomorrow we… we make it stop." He looked at the hill, toward the curtained house, and he said, "Tomorrow."

And they went on, down the long way, toward the house, toward the home, and the hum followed, patient and keeping, and the town was preparing for the first day of fall, and the picnic was the answer, and the love was still there, even after eight years, even with the curtains closed.

And when they got home, the narrow house, the house with the creaky middle stair and the refrigerator that hummed like it knew a secret, Ollie put Captain Chomp under his chin, the way he always did, the way he had on the first night, and he said, "You're home. You're home. You're home. And you're never going to be lost again. I promise. I'll label you. I'll label you so you never get lost again."

And Pip, who was eleven, who had a label maker, who had decided on the first night that nothing in this family would ever get lost, took out the label maker, and he printed a label, and he pressed it onto Captain Chomp's foot, gently, the way you press a label onto a thing that is not going anywhere. And the label said:

CAPTAIN CHOMP. HOME. COMES BACK TO.

And Ollie looked at the label, and he looked at Pip, and he said, "You labeled him. You labeled the captain."

"I labeled him," said Pip. "So he knows where home is. So he never forgets the way again."

And Ollie, who had said *you don't label a person, he's not a thing, he's Captain Chomp*, looked at the label, and he looked at the dinosaur, and he said, "Okay. Okay, you can label him. Because… because the label's not a thing. The label's a promise. The label's a… the label's a spare button. The label says I expect you to come loose, and I expect to fix you, and I am not going anywhere." He looked at Pip with his whole face. "That's what the label says. That's what Thimble said. The spare is a promise. And the label's a spare. And the label's a promise."

And Pip, who was eleven, who had a label maker, who had just heard his six-year-old brother say *the label's a promise*, felt something go warm in his chest, the rearranging, the furniture-moving, the thing he did not have a label for yet. And he thought: *the love stays. Even when the thing is lost. Even when the thing is kept. Even when the thing is gone. The love stays. And the label is the love. And the label is the promise. And the label is the thing we're going to show the man on the hill.*

And tomorrow was Finding Day. And tomorrow, they were going to prove it.

But tonight, there was a dinosaur under a chin, and a label that said HOME, COMES BACK TO, and a long way home that was actually a shortcut, and a hum that followed, patient and keeping.

And tonight, that was a start.
