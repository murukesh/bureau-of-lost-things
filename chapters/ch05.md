## Chapter 5 — The Pocket of Useless Junk

The form was four pages long and asked, among other things, for the dinosaur's name, rank, and distinguishing marks, and Pip was filling in *secret pirate* under OCCUPATION when the door opened again and Goldie Finch came in, eating an apple.

"Hey," she said. "You found it. I figured you would. You've got the look."

Pip stared at her. "You can see the door?"

"Obviously I can see the door." Goldie took a bite of apple. "I've been coming here since I was seven. Gran used to bring me. She'd lose a button on purpose just to get the door to show, and then she'd say hello to Barnaby and have a biscuit and go home. It was her whole social life." She nodded at the walrus. "Hi, Barnaby."

"Miss Finch," said Barnaby, warmly, leaking. "A pleasure, as always. You are just in time for the form."

"I'm never just in time for the form," said Goldie. "I'm allergic to the form. I'll be over here." She wandered off toward the shelves with the easy, shortcutting walk of a girl who knew every aisle, and Pip, abandoning OCCUPATION, went after her, because he had a question that had been growing in him since the driveway.

"How?" he said. "The door. It only shows if you've lost something you truly love. But you—" He gestured at her pocket, which jingled. "You lose things all the time. You don't even mind. You lost your good acorn yesterday and you just shrugged."

Goldie stopped at a shelf of single mittens and looked at him. For a second the easy, shortcutting look went off her face, and underneath it was something older and quieter.

"I love my stuff," she said. "I just don't mind when it goes. Those are two different things, Pip. People think they're the same thing. They're not."

She put the apple core in her pocket — Pip watched her do it, watched the pocket accept it without complaint — and then she turned the pocket half inside out, the way she had at the house, and let the contents spill into her palm.

"Exhibit A," she said. "The pocket of useless junk."

There, in her hand, were three things. A marble, a paperclip, and a rubber band.

The marble was glass, a cat's-eye, a swirl of green and gold trapped inside it like a small frozen weather. The paperclip was silver and bent, bent the way a paperclip gets when it has been picked at and twisted and thought with. The rubber band was red, and a little cracked, and stretched out of shape from a life of holding things together that were not its job to hold.

"This," said Goldie, holding up the marble, "is a marble. It's useless. You can't eat it, you can't wear it, it's no good for anything except rolling, and it's always rolling under things. I've had it since I was six. I love it." She set it on the shelf, and it rolled an inch, and she stopped it with one finger without looking. "This," holding up the paperclip, "is a paperclip. It's bent. It's useless. It can't hold paper anymore, not really. I love it." She hooked it onto her collar. "And this," holding up the rubber band, "is a rubber band. It's cracked. It's useless. It's about to snap, honestly, any day now. I love it."

Pip looked at the three useless things. "Why do you love them if they're useless?"

Goldie looked at him like he'd asked why the sky was up. "Because they're mine," she said. "Because I've had them. Because the marble's got a chip on it from the time I dropped it on the church steps, and the paperclip's bent because I bent it thinking about my dad, and the rubber band held my hair the day I learned to ride a bike. Useless isn't the opposite of loved, Pip. Useless is just what a thing is after it's done being useful. The loving's still in there."

She put the marble back in the pocket, and the paperclip, and the rubber band, and the pocket jingled its small, satisfied jingle, and Pip felt something in his chest rearrange itself, quietly, like furniture being moved in a room he hadn't known he had.

"You're going to lose them someday," he said. It wasn't a question. He was eleven. He had decided things. He knew how this went.

"Probably," said Goldie, and she smiled, and the smile was the calmest thing Pip had ever seen. "The marble'll roll under something I can't reach. The paperclip'll fall out of a hole in this pocket. The rubber band'll snap." She patted the pocket. "And I'll be sad. And then I'll remember the church steps, and your dad, and the bike. And the sad'll be the good kind. The kind that means it was real."

Pip thought of Captain Chomp, being dusted somewhere in this enormous room. He thought of Ollie, over by the low shelf, not touching the waiting animals. He thought of a house with too many curtains, holding its breath, keeping everything, never letting anything go, never letting anything be the good kind of sad.

"I don't think I can do that," he said honestly. "The not-minding part."

"You don't have to," said Goldie. "Not yet. Maybe not ever. That's okay too." She hooked her arm through his, which was a very Goldie thing to do, a shortcut through a conversation. "Come on. I'll show you the good shelves. Barnaby'll do the form till the cows come home, and the cows, by the way, are also lost, we have a whole field of them, don't ask."

She led him down an aisle, and then another, taking turns Pip was sure didn't exist, past the umbrella orchard and the jar of humming keys, past a shelf of single earrings that glittered like a city at night, past a bin labeled LEFT SHOES, ALL OF THEM, WHY.

In a low, warm corner they came to a hedgehog. He was small and round and the color of toast, and he was standing on a stool in front of a shelf of single socks, holding one up to his nose with the gravity of a scientist holding a beaker. His quills were full of buttons — dozens of them, stuck in among the spines like fruit in a very prickly bush — and they clicked softly when he moved.

"That's Thimble," Goldie whispered. "He sorts the socks by smell. Don't interrupt him. He's doing the toast ones."

The hedgehog sniffed the sock, closed his eyes, and placed it gently into a bin labeled WARM / BREAKFAST / SOMEONE'S KITCHEN. Then he picked up another, sniffed, and frowned. "Rain," he murmured. "And dog. And a little bit of sadness. That's a difficult one." He set it aside in a bin labeled COMPLICATED, and only then did he notice them, and give a small, formal nod, and go back to his smelling.

"He's been here longer than anyone," Goldie said, as they moved on. "He says every sock smells like the person who lost it. He says if you smell hard enough you can find your way home." She said it lightly, but Pip filed it, the way he filed everything, in a drawer he was starting to suspect he would need.

And as they walked, Goldie talked, and the Bureau unfolded around them, and Pip — who had come here to find a dinosaur — began, slowly, to find something else, something he didn't have a label for yet, something about the difference between a thing that is lost and a thing that is gone, and the difference between loving a thing and keeping it, and how the pocket of a girl who didn't mind could hold more than a house with too many curtains.

"We'll get him back," Goldie said, quietly, like she'd been reading the back of his head. "Captain Chomp. That's what this place is for. Things come home here. That's the whole point of the whole place." She squeezed his arm once, let go, and pointed her apple-less hand down the aisle like a captain. "Now come on. Barnaby's about to explain Finding Day, and he uses props."

Behind them, at the desk, Barnaby stamped the form with a flipper, and the stamp said RECEIVED, and the ink was a little smudged, because the pen was still leaking, and because some things, in the Bureau of Lost Things, are allowed to be a little smudged and still be true.
