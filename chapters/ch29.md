## Chapter 29 — Captain Chomp Reports for Duty

Captain Chomp reported for duty at six o'clock, which was dinner time, and reporting for duty meant sitting at the table in his own chair, which Ollie had made for him out of a shoebox and a great deal of tape, and it meant being served a bite of everything, and it meant roaring, once, at full volume, to announce that he was home and that he was a secret pirate and that the hat was invisible.

"He's home," Ollie announced, to the table, to the house, to the town, to the world. "Captain Chomp's home. He's home. He's home. And he's reporting for duty. And he's never going to be lost again. I labeled him. Pip labeled him. The label's a promise. The label says I expect you to come loose, and I expect to fix you, and I am not going anywhere."

"That's a very good label," said Pip's mother, who was setting down the potatoes, and who looked at the dinosaur in the shoebox chair, and who looked at Ollie, and who looked at Pip, and who had the tired-and-proud look, but more proud than tired now, much more proud than tired. "That's a very good promise."

"It's Thimble's promise," said Ollie. "Thimble's a hedgehog. He sorts the socks by smell. And he gives spare buttons to lost coats. And he's prepared for loss. And he's not going anywhere." He fed Captain Chomp a bite of potato. "And the label's a spare. And the label's a promise. And I'm not going anywhere either. I'm the Official Counter. I count to zero. And zero means nothing's missing."

Pip's father, who was wrestling a serving spoon, which was a thing he did, which was a thing he always did, looked up and said, "Who's Thimble?" And then he said, "Wait. Don't tell me. It's a long story. It's a… it's a Bureau story." He looked at Pip. "You've been… you've been somewhere. You've been somewhere good. I can tell. You've got the… you've got the look. The look of a boy who's found a place."

And Pip, who was eleven, who had a label maker, who had come to Marrow's End feeling like a lost thing, who had labeled his shoe STILL HERE because he had not been sure it was true, felt something go warm in his chest, because his father had seen it. His father had seen the thing he had not been able to say, which was that he had found a place, that he was not a lost thing anymore, that he was a Friend of the Lost, that he had a job and a title and a cat who corrected his spelling and a hedgehog who gave him spare buttons and a walrus who made up contracts and a girl who took him through fences.

"I found a place," said Pip. "I found… I found the Bureau. The Bureau of Lost Things. And I'm a Friend of the Lost. It's not official. Barnaby made it up. But it's true." He looked at his father. "And I'm not… I'm not a lost thing anymore. I was. I was a lost thing. When we moved. I was a lost thing. But I'm not. I'm found. I'm… I'm a keeper. I'm one of the keepers."

And Pip's father, who wrestled furniture and usually lost, set down the serving spoon, and he looked at Pip, and he said, "Well. Well, look at that. The boy's a keeper. The boy's found a place." He reached across the table, and he put his hand on Pip's shoulder, and he said, "I'm glad. I'm glad you found a place. I'm… I'm glad you're not a lost thing anymore. Because you were never… you were never unloved, Pip. You were never unloved. You were just… you were just new. And new is a kind of lost. And lost is a kind of fog. But the loving was the ground under the fog. It was there the whole time. We were… we were here the whole time."

And Pip, who was eleven, who had a label maker, who had just heard his father say *the loving was the ground under the fog*, felt the thing go warm in his chest, the rearranging, the furniture-moving, because his father had said the thing he had learned at the Bureau, the thing he had learned on the lost step, the thing about feeling lost not being the same as being unloved. His father had known it the whole time. His father had been the ground under the fog the whole time. And Pip had just… Pip had just needed to find the Bureau to see it.

They ate. They ate the potatoes and the roast and the peas, and Captain Chomp was served a bite of everything, and he roared, once, at full volume, and the hat was invisible, and the homecoming was a homecoming, and the narrow house, the house with the creaky middle stair and the refrigerator that hummed like it knew a secret, was a home, was a home, was a home.

And then the hum came.

It was faint, at the edge of things, under the hill, under the town, a low, steady, keeping note, and it came up through the floor of the narrow house, and Pip felt it, and his mother felt it, and his father felt it, and Ollie felt it, and Captain Chomp, in his shoebox chair, seemed to lean away from it, just a little, just a tremor.

"What's that?" said Pip's mother. "That hum. What's that hum?"

"The Keeper," said Pip. "It's… it's the Keeper. It's the machine. The man on the hill. Mr. Snell. He built a machine so nothing he loves is ever lost again. And it… it keeps. It keeps everything. And tomorrow, on Finding Day, it's going to keep everything. Everything that's lost. Everything that's loved. And we're going to… we're going to answer it. We're going to show it the love stays."

And Pip's mother, who was a polite person, who had the tired-and-proud look, looked at Pip, and she said, "The man on the hill. The Snell place. The house with too many curtains." She paused, and her face did a thing, a remembering thing. "I knew him. Before. I knew Silas. He used to… he used to come to the school fairs. He used to make things. He made… he made a music box once, for the fair. And it played a waltz. And everyone danced. And he… he was kind. He was very kind. And then the dog ran off, and he… he stopped coming. He stopped coming to everything." She looked at Pip. "And you're going to help him? You're going to… you're going to answer the heartbreak?"

"We're going to try," said Pip. "Tomorrow. On Finding Day. We're going to show him the love stays. We're going to show him he can let go and still have the love. And we're going to get the things home. The eighteen. The music box. The telescope. The harmonica. The red mitten. We're going to get them home."

And Pip's mother, who was a polite person, who had decided a long time ago to be kind, reached across the table, and she put her hand on Pip's hand, and she said, "Then I'll bring a thing I love. To the picnic. Tomorrow. I'll bring a thing I love. And I'll… I'll leave the bowl out. That's the job. You leave the bowl out, and the things come home." She looked at Pip with the tired-and-proud look, all proud now, all proud. "You leave the bowl out, Pip. That's the whole thing. You leave the bowl out."

And the hum went on, patient and keeping, under the hill, under the town, and the Keeper was getting ready for Finding Day, and the Keeper was going to keep everything, and the Keeper had let one thing go and did not like it, and the Keeper was going to try again.

But the narrow house was a home, and the dinosaur was under a chin, and the label said HOME, COMES BACK TO, and the family was together, and the loving was the ground under the fog, and it was there the whole time, and it was there now.

And tomorrow was Finding Day. And tomorrow, they were going to prove that the love stays.

But tonight, there was a roast, and a shoebox chair, and a secret pirate with an invisible hat, and a father who wrestled serving spoons, and a mother who had decided to leave the bowl out.

And tonight, that was a start.
