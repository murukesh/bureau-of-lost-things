## Chapter 6 - Form 27-B: Missing, Possibly Forever

Barnaby did, in fact, use props.

He produced, from somewhere under the desk, a snow globe. Inside the snow globe was a tiny green door. He set it on the desk between them with the ceremony of a magician producing a dove, and then he produced a second prop, which was a biscuit, which he ate, because explaining made him peckish.

"Right," he said, crumbs on his whiskers. "The rules. There are three. I will say them once, and then I will say them again, because I am a walrus and we are thorough."

Pip sat up straight. Ollie sat up straighter. Goldie leaned against the desk, already bored, already knowing this part.

"Rule one," said Barnaby, holding up a flipper. "You can only see the door if you have lost something you truly love. Not something you merely like. Not something you are merely inconvenienced by. Something you love. The door knows the difference. The door is very good at its job." He tapped the snow globe. "You three have all lost something you love. That is why you are here. That is why the door showed itself. It is not a magic trick. It is a sympathy."

"Rule two," he continued, holding up the other flipper, which still had a little biscuit on it. "A thing that is lost is not gone. It is waiting. We keep it safe. We dust it. We help it remember." He leaned forward, and his voice went softer, and the whole enormous room seemed to lean in with him. "Because here is the thing about being lost, and it is the hard thing, so I will say it gently. When a thing is lost, it starts to forget. It forgets the way home. And if it stays lost too long - if it is not claimed, if it is not loved back - then the person who lost it starts to forget too. Not the thing. The *loving*. They forget how much they loved it. And then the thing is not lost anymore. It is just... gone."

The room was very quiet. Ollie's hand found Pip's again.

"That's why we wait with them," said Barnaby. "So they don't forget. So the loving stays warm. A thing that is waited with remembers. A thing that is loved back finds its way." He looked at Ollie. "Captain Chomp is being waited with. He is being loved back. He has not forgotten you. He roars in his sleep. The staff find it very comforting."

Ollie's face did the complicated thing again, but this time it landed somewhere warm. "He does?" he whispered.

"He does," said Barnaby. "A secret pirate's roar. Very fierce. Very small."

"Rule three," said Barnaby, and he set down the snow globe and folded his flippers, and for the first time he looked not just kind but serious, in the way that kind people look when they are about to tell you the true thing. "Once a year, on the first day of fall, we open the big door. Not the little green one you came through. The big one. The one at the back that none of you have seen yet. And on that day, every lost thing that is ready - every thing that has been claimed and loved back and remembers the way - gets to go home. All at once. One day. We call it Finding Day."

"When is it?" said Pip.

Barnaby produced a third prop, which was a calendar, and it was a calendar of autumn leaves, and he turned it to a page with a single leaf circled in red.

"Six days from today," he said. "The first day of fall. Finding Day. It is the one day the way home is open and clear. The rest of the year the way is... difficult. Twisted. A lost thing can wander for months and not find it. But on Finding Day, the door is wide, and the road is straight, and everything that is ready goes home." He looked at Pip. "Captain Chomp was very lost when he came in. Frightened. He has forgotten a good deal of the way. He needs the week to remember. And he needs to be claimed, and loved back, and helped. That is your job, if you want it."

"I want it," said Pip, so fast it surprised him.

"I want it too," said Ollie, at full volume, which was his way of signing a contract.

"Then you will come," said Barnaby. "Every day, if you like. You will sit with him. You will tell him about home. You will remind him of the grape juice and the chin and the roaring. You will love him back. And on Finding Day, if he is ready, he will go home with you." He paused, and his small eyes were very gentle. "Most things are ready. Most things go home. It is a good day. The best day."

Pip heard the *most*. He was eleven. He heard the word that was doing the quiet work in that sentence, the word that meant *not all*, the word that meant there was a reason Barnaby had said rule two so gently. He thought of Miss Whisk's *and every thing that doesn't, which lately is a longer list than it ought to be.* He filed it, and he did not say it out loud, because Ollie was signing contracts at full volume and did not need the *most* right now.

"What happens," Pip asked carefully, "to the things that aren't ready?"

Barnaby was quiet for a moment. "They wait another year," he said. "We keep them safe. We keep loving them back. And we hope." He did not say the rest, the *and if they wait too long the loving goes out*, because he had already said it gently once and did not believe in saying hard things twice to a six-year-old. But Pip heard it anyway, in the shape of the silence.

"Okay," said Pip. "Then we'll make sure he's ready."

Barnaby beamed the large, damp beaming. "Splendid. Now. The rest of the form." He pushed the four pages toward Pip. "Sign here. And here. And initial the dinosaur's rank. And I will need a description of the home, for the file. The more love in the description, the better. The way home is paved with the details."

So Pip wrote. He wrote about the narrow house and the creaky middle stair and the refrigerator that hummed like it knew a secret. He wrote about the pizza on the floor and the box labeled TABLE, LEGS, PROBABLY. He wrote about Ollie's wrong-footed brave shoes and the duck sock that smelled like toast and the grape-juice smell and the chin and the roaring. He wrote until the description of home was a page long, and then, because Barnaby said the more love the better, he wrote a little more.

When he was done, Barnaby stamped it, and the stamp said RECEIVED, and he filed it in a drawer that was exactly the size of the paper, because the Bureau was like that.

"Welcome to the Bureau, Pip Parker," said Barnaby. "You are now a Friend of the Lost. It is not an official title. I just made it up. But it is true."

"He makes up a lot of titles," Goldie said. "I'm a Knight of the Shortcut. It's not official either. There's no sword. I asked."

"There is a sword," said Barnaby, with dignity. "It is lost. We are keeping it for you."

Pip looked at the stamp, and at the snow globe with the tiny green door, and at Ollie, who was already planning his first visit out loud, at full volume, to a dinosaur who was somewhere in this enormous, kindly room, roaring in his sleep, being waited with, not forgotten.

One week. Finding Day. They had one week to love a dinosaur back to remembering.

Pip decided, then and there, that he was going to be the best Friend of the Lost this Bureau had ever seen. He did not know, yet, that something in the Bureau was taking things in the night, and that the list of the not-ready was growing, and that a lonely man on a hill was building a machine so that nothing he loved would ever be lost again.

He only knew that he had a week, and a form, and a brother, and a small green dinosaur who roared in his sleep.

It was, he thought, enough to start with.
