## Chapter 10 - The Umbrella Orchard (Please Do Not Water)

By the end of the third day, Pip had a job.

He had not meant to get a job. He had meant to love Captain Chomp back to remembering and then go home. But the Bureau was enormous and the staff were few, and there is a law - older than forms, older than the door - that when a willing boy stands in a room full of work, the work finds him.

It started with the dusting. Barnaby handed him a cloth and said, "The things like to be dusted. It reminds them they are still things. A thing that is not dusted begins to feel like a memory. We do not want that." So Pip dusted. He dusted the single gloves and the jar of humming keys and the spectacle frames with no lenses, and he found that dusting was not cleaning, exactly. It was a way of saying *I see you. You are still here. You are still a thing.* And the things, he could have sworn, stood a little straighter under the cloth.

Then it was the labeling. Miss Whisk, who did not like visitors, took one look at Pip's label maker and said, "Huh. A machine that makes the record smaller." And she let him - actually let him - make tags for the new intake, because her paw cramped in the evenings and she would not admit it. Pip printed tags in his careful block letters, and Miss Whisk corrected his spelling without looking, and they worked in a silence that was, Pip realized, the comfortable silence of two people who both believed that writing a thing down was a way of keeping it.

And then it was the waiting.

This was the part Pip had not understood, the part Barnaby had said was the important part. Every thing in the Bureau needed to be waited with. Not all the time. But some of the time. A thing that was waited with remembered. So Pip sat with them. He sat with the red mitten and told it about the grandmother who had knitted it, which he did not know, so he made up a grandmother who was probably close, and the mitten seemed to like it. He sat with the toy car with one wheel and told it about being small, which he did know, and the car's one wheel turned a little, as if remembering a specific carpet. He sat with the spectacle frame and read to it, the way the man used to read to his dog, and he read the labels on the nearby shelves, which was not a story, but was, he thought, a kind of story, the story of all the things that were waiting.

And in between, he saw the wonders.

He saw the umbrella orchard, which was a whole grove of furled umbrellas standing in rows like trees, and when the light hit them they made a sound like rain that wasn't there, and the sign said PLEASE DO NOT WATER, and Goldie told him that once a pigeon had watered them out of spite and they had all bloomed at once and it had taken a week to get them furled again, and Barnaby had been very diplomatic about it but his whiskers had never forgiven the pigeon.

He saw the field of lost cows. It was actually a field, in a room that should not have fit a field, and the cows were gray and patient and mooed softly, and they were all the cows that had ever been lost, which was more cows than Pip would have guessed, and they were waiting for the farmers who had lost them, and some of the farmers had been gone a long time, and the cows did not know that, and the Bureau did not tell them, because the waiting was the kind part.

He saw the jar of keys, which hummed when you passed it, and he asked Barnaby why, and Barnaby said, "They are remembering their locks. A key without a lock is a question. The hum is the question." And Pip thought that was the saddest and most beautiful thing he had ever heard, and he put his hand on the jar, and the hum went through his palm and up his arm, and he felt, for a second, every locked door in Marrow's End, and every person who had ever stood in front of one, patting their pockets.

He saw the drawer of homework excuses, valid and dubious, and he laughed, and Miss Whisk said, "The dubious ones are more honest. The valid ones are trying too hard," and Pip laughed harder.

And every day, twice a day, he sat with Captain Chomp, and Ollie sat with him, and they told the dinosaur about home, about the middle stair and the refrigerator and the pizza on the floor, and Captain Chomp's loose button caught the light a little more each day, and his twice-sewn tail seemed a little less unsure, and once - once - when Ollie did the roar, the dinosaur's whole body seemed to lean into it, the way a plant leans into the sun.

"He's remembering," Barnaby said, watching. "You can see it. The loving is paving the way."

And Pip, dusting and labeling and waiting, realized something that he had to sit down on a lost step (STEP. STILL LOOKING.) to think about.

He had come to Marrow's End feeling like a lost thing. He had labeled his shoe STILL HERE because he had not been sure it was true. He had labeled the house HOME because he had not been sure it was. He had been a thing set down somewhere and not picked back up, a single sock in a town of dryers, waiting to be claimed.

And now he had a job. He had a title, Friend of the Lost, which was not official but was true. He had a cat who corrected his spelling and a hedgehog who gave him spare buttons and a walrus who made up contracts and a girl who took him through fences. He had a dinosaur to love back and a brother to be brave for and a bowl to remember and a record to keep.

He was not a lost thing anymore. He was a thing that had been found. Not by being claimed - nobody had claimed Pip. But by being useful. By being waited with. By having a place in the enormous, kindly, slightly chaotic room where the lost things were kept, and by being, himself, one of the keepers.

Feeling lost, he understood now, was not the same as being unloved. He had felt lost, and he had been loved the whole time - by his mother with the tired-and-proud look, by his father who wrestled chairs, by Ollie who put his shoes on the wrong feet on purpose. The lost feeling had been a fog. The loving had been the ground under the fog. It had been there the whole time. He just hadn't been able to see it, because he had been new, and new is a kind of lost, and lost is a kind of fog.

He sat on the lost step and he felt the ground under the fog, and it was solid, and it was warm, and it was his.

"Pip," said Ollie, appearing with Captain Chomp under his arm, because it was going-home time. "Ready?"

"Ready," said Pip.

They said goodnight to Barnaby, who was leaking contentedly, and to Miss Whisk, who said, "Don't let the door hit you. It's shy, but it's also heavy," and to Thimble, who was doing the rain ones and did not look up but raised one quill in salute, the buttons clicking. And they went to the little green door, and Pip put his hand on the brass knob, and he paused.

"Can I label it?" he asked. "The door. So I can find it again. So I know it's here."

Barnaby considered this. "The door finds you," he said. "You know that. You don't need a label."

"I know," said Pip. "But I want to give it one. Not so I can find it. So it knows it's found."

Barnaby's small eyes went very soft. "Yes," he said. "I think that's allowed. I think that's very allowed."

So Pip printed a label, and he pressed it onto the little green door, right under the brass plate that said BUREAU OF LOST THINGS. PLEASE KNOCK. And the label said:

FOUND. COMES BACK TO.

He looked at it, and it looked back, and he felt the ground under the fog, solid and warm.

Then they stepped through, into the schoolyard, into the dusk, into Marrow's End, and the door swung shut behind them, and the smell of dust and lavender and old paper went out like a candle.

Pip did not notice, then, that the label was slightly crooked. He did not notice that it would matter, later, that it was crooked, that a crooked label on a door that moves is a thing you can follow. He only noticed that the door was green, and that it was found, and that he would be back tomorrow.

And from somewhere under the hill, very faintly, something hummed. It was a patient hum. A keeping hum. The hum of a machine that had decided, quietly, that nothing it loved would ever be lost again.

Pip heard it, and stopped, and looked down the hill.

"Pip?" said Ollie. "What is it?"

"Nothing," said Pip. "I thought I heard... nothing."

They went home. And the humming went on, patient and keeping, under the hill, under the house with too many curtains, getting ready.

But that was tomorrow's problem. Tonight there was a dinosaur who was almost remembering, and a label that said FOUND, and a boy who had stopped being a lost thing.

Tonight, that was enough.
