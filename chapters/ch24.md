## Chapter 24 - Watch Me

They brought the thing about the warm, and the thing about the warm was Goldie's pocket.

That was the plan, the one they had made in the lane, the one that Ollie had improved by saying *just show him the pocket, the pocket knows things*, and the plan was this: they would show Snell the pocket, the marble and the paperclip and the rubber band, the three useless things that were loved and were not kept, and they would show him the difference, and the difference would be the crack, and the crack would be where the light got in.

So they went back, on the seventh day, in the afternoon, and Snell made tea, and he made the dog kind, and he set them on the plate, and he looked at the plate, and Pip saw the look, and filed it, and did not say anything. And they sat in the keeping room, among the shelves and the kept things, and the Keeper hummed under the floor, and Goldie turned her pocket half inside out, the way she did, and let the contents spill into her palm.

"Exhibit A," she said. "The pocket of useless junk."

There, in her hand, were the three things. The marble, the cat's-eye, the swirl of green and gold. The paperclip, silver and bent. The rubber band, red and cracked and stretched.

"Mr. Snell," said Goldie. "These are mine. They're useless. You can't eat them, you can't wear them, they're no good for anything. And I love them. I love them a lot. The marble's got a chip from the church steps. The paperclip's bent because I bent it thinking about my dad. The rubber band held my hair the day I learned to ride a bike." She held them out, and the lamplight caught them, and they were small and useless and loved. "And here's the thing, Mr. Snell. Here's the whole thing. I love them. And I don't mind when they go."

Snell looked at the three things. "You... you don't mind?"

"I don't mind," said Goldie. "The marble'll roll under something I can't reach. The paperclip'll fall out of a hole in this pocket. The rubber band'll snap. And I'll be sad. And then I'll remember the church steps, and my dad, and the bike. And the sad'll be the good kind. The kind that means it was real." She closed her hand around the three things, and the pocket jingled the small, satisfied jingle. "I love them. And I let them go. And the love stays. That's the thing. That's the whole thing. The love stays, even when the thing goes. The love stays."

Snell was quiet for a long moment. He looked at the three useless things, and he looked at his shelves, at the kept things, at the music boxes that were not playing and the books that were not read and the gloves that were not worn, and his face was the face of a man who is being shown a thing he has been avoiding for eight years.

"But... but what if they're gone forever?" he said. "What if the marble rolls under something, and you never see it again? What if the paperclip falls out, and it's gone? What if the rubber band snaps, and it's... and it's gone? What then? What then, Goldie? What do you do then?"

"Then I remember," said Goldie. "Then I remember the church steps, and my dad, and the bike. And the remembering is the love. And the love stays. Even when the thing is gone. The love stays." She looked at Snell with her shortcut eyes, her kind, calm, shortcut eyes. "Mr. Snell. You can't keep everything you love. You can't. Nobody can. The things go. That's what things do. And if you try to keep everything, you... you don't get to love them. You just get to keep them. And keeping is not loving. Keeping is... keeping is being afraid, with a machine."

And Snell, who was a man shaped like an umbrella, who had been alone eight years, who had built a machine so nothing he loves is ever lost again, looked at the girl with the pocket of useless junk, and the crack opened, wider than it had ever been, and for a long moment he did not speak. And then he said, in a voice that was soft and broken and wondering, "You can't keep everything you love."

"No," said Goldie. "You can't."

"You can't keep everything you love," said Snell, and he said it again, the way you say a thing you are trying to believe, the way you say a thing that is true and is a relief and is a terror, all at once. And then he did the thing that was funny and was sad and was the whole heartbreak in one gesture. He stood up, and he looked at his shelves, at the kept things, at the Keeper humming under the floor, and he said, "Watch me."

"Watch me," said Silas Snell, the saddest man in town, the man who had built a machine so nothing he loves is ever lost again. "Watch me. I'm going to keep everything. I'm going to keep everything I love. And I'm going to prove it. I'm going to prove that you can keep everything you love. Watch me."

And the Keeper hummed, patient and keeping, and the hum was loud, and the hum was proud, and the hum was the hum of a machine that has been told *watch me* and has decided to rise to the occasion. And Pip felt the ground under the fog go cold, because *watch me* was not the answer. *Watch me* was the heartbreak doubling down. *Watch me* was the man who had been shown the crack and had decided to build a bigger wall. And the Keeper was going to rise to the occasion. And the Keeper was going to keep everything. And the Keeper was going to prove it.

"Mr. Snell," said Pip. "That's not... that's not the point. The point is not to keep everything. The point is to love everything. And loving is not keeping. Loving is... loving is letting the thing be a thing. Letting it be warm. Letting it risk being lost. The point is not to prove you can keep everything. The point is to... to let the love stay, even when the thing goes."

"Watch me," said Snell, and he was not listening, and he was not being mean, and he was being gentle and heartbroken and afraid, and he had decided to build a bigger wall. "Watch me. I'm going to keep everything. And on Finding Day... on Finding Day, the Keeper is going to keep everything. Everything that's lost. Everything that's loved. Everything. And nothing will ever be lost again. And I'll prove it. I'll prove that you can keep everything you love."

Pip felt the shape of the problem settle into him, hard and clear and cold. The man was not going to let go. The man was going to double down. The man was going to keep everything, on Finding Day, and the Keeper was going to rise to the occasion, and the Keeper was going to keep everything, and nothing would ever be lost again, and nothing would ever be warm again, and nothing would ever go home.

"Mr. Snell," said Pip. "Please. Please don't. The things... the things want to go home. The people are waiting. The people are going to forget. Please. Let them go home. Let the love stay. Please."

And Snell, who was a man shaped like an umbrella, who had been alone eight years, looked at Pip with his kind, tired eyes, and the crack was there, wide, wider than it had ever been, and for a long moment Pip thought the man was going to say yes. And then the man said, "I'll make you a deal. I'll... I'll let one go home. One thing. The dinosaur. I'll let the dinosaur go home. Because... because the boy has a dinosaur-shaped hole. And I know the hole. And I'll let the dinosaur go home." He paused, and his voice went soft. "But just the one. Just the dinosaur. The rest... the rest I'm keeping. The rest I'm keeping. Watch me."

And that was the deal. One dinosaur. One thing back. The rest kept. The rest kept, on Finding Day, when the Keeper was going to keep everything, and nothing would ever be lost again, and nothing would ever be warm again.

Pip felt the ground under the fog go cold and solid. The deal was a trap and a kindness and the heartbreak. It was going to get Captain Chomp home, and keep everything else, and it was not the answer.

But it was a start. It was a crack. It was one dinosaur. And one dinosaur was a brother's hole, filled. And one dinosaur was a start.

"We'll take the dinosaur," said Pip. "But we're not done. We're not done, Mr. Snell. We're going to show you. We're going to show you the love stays. And we're going to do it on Finding Day. And you're going to see. You're going to see that you can't keep everything you love. And you're going to see that the love stays anyway."

"Watch me," said Snell, softly, to himself, the way you say a thing you are trying to believe.

And they went out into the lane, into the dusk, and the house with too many curtains held its breath, and the Keeper hummed, patient and keeping, rising to the occasion, getting ready for Finding Day.

And tomorrow was Finding Day. And the Keeper was going to keep everything. And Pip was going to prove that the love stays.

But tonight, there was a deal, and one dinosaur, and a man saying *watch me* to himself, softly, the way you say a thing you are trying to believe.

And tonight, that was a start.
