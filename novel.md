# PIP PARKER AND THE BUREAU OF LOST THINGS

## Chapter 1 - The Boy Who Labeled the Refrigerator

The moving truck smelled like cardboard and other people's lives, and Pip Parker decided, before he had even carried in the first box, that this house was not going to swallow him.

He was eleven, which is a good age for deciding things, and he had a label maker, which is a good tool for enforcing them. The label maker was red and shaped like a small, serious sandwich, and it printed letters that stuck to anything. Pip had bought it with his own money, three birthdays of it, and he loved it the way some kids love a bike or a dog. It made the world hold still.

"Everything gets a label," he told the empty hallway. "Then nothing gets lost."

The hallway did not argue. It was a narrow hallway in a narrow house in a town called Marrow's End, and it had a staircase that creaked in the middle like it was clearing its throat. Pip pressed a label onto the newel post.

STAIRS. WATCH THE MIDDLE ONE.

He pressed one onto the front door.

FRONT DOOR. COMES BACK TO.

He pressed one onto the refrigerator, which had come with the house and hummed like it knew a secret.

FRIDGE. COLD. MOSTLY.

His mother appeared in the kitchen doorway holding a lamp with no shade. "Pip," she said, "the refrigerator does not need a name."

"It's not a name," said Pip. "It's a location."

His mother looked at him for a moment, the way she had been looking at him a lot since they moved, like she was checking whether he was still all there. Then she smiled, a little tired, a little proud, and went to find the lampshade. Pip felt something warm and sore in his chest at the same time. That was the thing about being new. Every room was a question you hadn't answered yet.

His father was in the backyard wrestling a folding chair. His father wrestled most furniture and usually lost. "Pip!" he called. "Come hold this while I figure out which way is up!"

"Up is the way with the seat," Pip called back, but he went and held it anyway.

That was the first day. They ate pizza on the floor because the table was still in a box labeled TABLE, LEGS, PROBABLY. Ollie sat cross-legged with a slice of pizza in one hand and a stuffed dinosaur in the other, and he fed the dinosaur a bite of crust every time he took one himself.

Ollie was six. He had a gap in his teeth you could mail a letter through, and he was brave in small, loud ways - the kind of brave that announces itself. He was not brave about the dark, or about the creaky stair, or about the first day of school, which was tomorrow. But he was brave about saying exactly what he felt, at full volume, whenever he felt it.

"This is Captain Chomp," Ollie said, holding up the dinosaur. "He's a pirate."

Captain Chomp was green, or had been green once. He was the green of a thing that has been loved until the color gives up. One of his button eyes was loose and the other was a different button entirely, a story Ollie told often and with variations. His tail had been sewn back on twice. He smelled, faintly and permanently, of grape juice and sleep.

"He's not a pirate," said Pip. "He has no hat."

"He's a secret pirate," said Ollie, with the patience of someone explaining the obvious to a large, slow person. "The hat is invisible."

Pip looked at Captain Chomp. Captain Chomp looked back with his two mismatched buttons, and Pip had the odd feeling that the dinosaur was taking notes on him.

"He needs a label," Pip said.

Ollie pulled Captain Chomp close. "No."

"Just a little one. On his foot. So if he ever-"

"No." Ollie's voice went small and loud at the same time, which was his most dangerous setting. "He's not a thing. He's Captain Chomp. You don't label a person."

Pip opened his mouth, then closed it. He had a whole argument ready, about how labels were how you kept things, how a label was a promise that said *this matters, this belongs, this comes back*. But Ollie was hugging the dinosaur with his whole face pressed into its stomach, and Pip could see the top of his brother's head, and he decided the argument could wait.

"Okay," Pip said. "No label on the captain."

Ollie relaxed by degrees, like a fist uncurling. "He already knows where home is," he said, quieter. "He doesn't need it written down."

Pip didn't say anything to that. He wasn't sure he agreed. He wasn't sure, lately, that he knew where home was either, and he was the one with the label maker.

Before dark, Pip took the recycling out to the curb, which was his job now, apparently, forever. He stood a moment at the end of the driveway and looked at Marrow's End.

It was a small town that had been folded up and put away carefully, like a sweater. The houses leaned toward each other as if sharing gossip. Down the hill there was a school with a brick wall and a wooden bin by the gate, and even from here Pip could read the sign on the bin: LOST AND FOUND. It was overflowing. A single red mitten hung off the edge like it was waving for help.

Next to the school was a bakery, warm light in its windows, and next to the bakery a lane that went uphill toward a tall house with too many curtains. Every window was curtained. Every curtain was drawn. Pip looked at that house a second longer than he meant to. It looked like a house that was holding its breath.

A girl came past him on a bike with no hands, a paper bag balanced on the handlebars. She had a pocket on her jacket that bulged like it was full of rocks, or treasures, or both. She caught him looking at the curtained house and slowed.

"Don't stare at the Snell place," she said, not unkindly. "It's rude. And also it's boring. Nothing ever happens there."

"I wasn't staring," said Pip.

"You were staring," said the girl, and put her hands back on the handlebars, which Pip thought was showing off, and rode away down the hill with the paper bag not falling off, which was definitely showing off.

Pip watched her go. He looked once more at the house with too many curtains, then at the school, then at the overflowing lost-and-found bin with its one red mitten, waving and waving. He had the strangest feeling, standing there in the dusk of his first day, that the town was full of things that had been set down somewhere and never picked back up. He didn't know why he thought it. He went inside and washed the pizza plates.

Later, when the boxes leaned against the walls like tired guests, Pip went around the house one more time. He labeled his room. He labeled Ollie's room. He labeled the closet where the vacuum lived (VACUUM. LOUD. SORRY.) and the drawer where the batteries went (BATTERIES. MAYBE DEAD.) and the windowsill where the light came in at four o'clock (LIGHT. ARRIVES 4:00.). He was not labeling the light because it needed it. He was labeling it because he wanted, just once, to know where something was going to be.

His mother found him on the stairs at the end, pressing a last label onto the banister.

HOME," she read. "You labeled the house HOME."

"It's a work in progress," said Pip.

She sat down next to him on the creaky middle step, which complained, and put her arm around him. "It's a lot, being new," she said. "It's okay if it feels like a lot."

"It's fine," said Pip, who was eleven and had decided things.

"It's okay if it's not fine," she said.

Pip leaned into her, just a little, just enough to be a leaning and not a collapse. Above them the house creaked and settled, getting used to them the way they were getting used to it. Somewhere down the hall Ollie was asleep with Captain Chomp tucked under his chin, the dinosaur's loose button eye catching the streetlight through the window, winking at nothing.

Pip looked at his label maker. He had one label left on the roll, a little white tongue of it sticking out, waiting.

He thought about what Ollie had said. *He already knows where home is. He doesn't need it written down.*

Pip hoped that was true. He hoped it was true for dinosaurs and for little brothers and for new kids with label makers, in narrow houses, in towns called Marrow's End, where every room was a question and every hallway smelled like cardboard and other people's lives.

He pressed the last label onto his own shoe.

PIP," it said. "STILL HERE."

Then he went to bed, and the house held still around him, and for one night, that was enough.

## Chapter 2 - The Town Where Dryers Eat Socks

The first load of laundry in the new house was the moving clothes, which smelled like the truck, which smelled like cardboard and other people's lives. Pip's mother loaded the washer, and Pip's father loaded the dryer, and Ollie loaded Captain Chomp into the dryer too, because Captain Chomp had been on the truck and therefore needed washing.

"Chomp goes in the washer," said Pip, rescuing the dinosaur. "The dryer will shrink him."

"He'll be fine," said Ollie.

"He'll be a raisin."

Ollie considered this. "A small brave raisin," he decided, and let Captain Chomp be carried to the washer, where he sat on top of the detergent like a figurehead on a very sudsy ship.

That was when Goldie Finch came over, because Goldie Finch was the kind of neighbor who came over. She arrived at the back door with a bowl of something covered in foil and a pocket that bulged, and she let herself in the way people do in small towns, like the door had been expecting her.

"Mom said you moved in," she said. "This is a welcome casserole. Don't ask what's in it. It's better if you don't ask."

Pip's mother, who was a polite person, took the casserole. "Thank you. I'm-"

"Mrs. Parker. I know. Everyone knows. You're the new people." Goldie said it the way you'd say *you're the new weather*. She was twelve, a year older than Pip, with scraped knees and a jacket whose pocket looked like it was smuggling a whole hardware store. "You're Pip. You label things."

Pip blinked. "How do you know that?"

"Because you labeled your shoe," said Goldie. "I saw it when you took the recycling out. It said STILL HERE. Which is a weird thing to put on a shoe, but I respect it."

Pip felt his ears go warm. Ollie, who had no such feelings about ears, marched up and held out Captain Chomp. "This is Captain Chomp. He's a secret pirate."

Goldie took the dinosaur seriously, which Pip noticed, and which made him decide he might like her. She examined the mismatched button eyes. "Good buttons," she said. "The loose one's got character. Don't ever fix that."

"I'm not going to fix it," said Ollie, scandalized.

"Good." Goldie handed Captain Chomp back. "Now. Whose dryer is that?"

Pip's father, who was proud of the dryer, said, "Ours. Why?"

"Because it's about to eat a sock," said Goldie. "They all do. It's the town."

And that was how Pip learned the first true thing about Marrow's End, which was not on any map and not in any welcome casserole.

Every dryer in town, Goldie explained, left exactly one lonely sock behind. Not two. Not none. One. You could put in twelve pairs and get out twenty-three socks, every time, and the twenty-third would be a stranger - a sock with no match, a sock that had never been yours, a sock that had simply *arrived*. And one of your own socks would be gone, gone forever, off to wherever the dryers sent them.

"It's a trade," said Goldie. "The dryers take one of yours and leave one of theirs. Nobody knows where the middle is. My mom says it's a plumbing thing. My dad says it's a raccoon thing. It's neither. It's just Marrow's End."

Pip looked at the dryer. The dryer hummed, innocent as a fridge.

"That's not possible," he said.

"Watch," said Goldie.

They watched. The dryer tumbled. The laundry went around and around, and Pip felt a little hypnotized by it, the way you do, and then his mother opened the door and pulled out the warm clothes and counted the socks onto the folding table.

Twenty-three.

One of them was a small gray sock with a duck on it. Nobody in the Parker family owned a duck sock. Ollie picked it up and held it to his face. "It smells like someone else's house," he said, and then, delighted, "It smells like *toast*."

Pip's father sat down slowly on a box labeled CHAIR, MAYBE. "I put in twelve pairs," he said. "I counted them. I'm a man who counts."

"Welcome to town," said Goldie.

She patted her bulging pocket, and her face did a small, familiar calculation. "Huh," she said. "My good acorn's gone."

"Your good acorn?" said Pip.

"I had a really good acorn. Perfect hat. No holes." Goldie turned the pocket half inside out. A paperclip, a marble, a rubber band, and a great deal of lint came to light, but no acorn. "Oh well. It was probably time."

"You're not going to look for it?" Pip could not imagine losing a good anything and not looking for it. He looked at his label maker on the counter, reassuringly.

Goldie shrugged, and the pocket jingled. "Things go. That's what things do. If you chase every single one you'll never get anywhere, and you'll be tired, and the acorn still won't be there." She pushed the lint back in. "Besides. I've got the important stuff." She patted the pocket, and the marble and the paperclip and the rubber band clicked together like a tiny, satisfied animal. "The rest is just the rest."

Pip thought this was possibly the least responsible thing he had ever heard, and also, strangely, the calmest. He filed it away, the way he filed everything, in a drawer he didn't have a label for yet.

Pip picked up the duck sock. It was warm. It was soft. It was, undeniably, a sock that belonged to someone, somewhere, and that someone was probably, at this very moment, looking for it. He thought of the lost-and-found bin by the school gate, overflowing, the red mitten waving. He thought of the house with too many curtains, holding its breath.

"Where do the lost ones go?" he asked.

Goldie shrugged, and her pocket jingled. "The Bureau, probably."

"The what?"

"The Bureau of Lost Things." She said it casually, like it was the post office. "It's where everything goes. Every lost thing in town. They file it and dust it and wait for it to be claimed. That's what my gran says, anyway. She says the dryers are just the front door."

Pip stared at her. "There's a bureau. For lost things."

"Sure." Goldie was already heading for the back door, casserole delivered, visit complete, a girl made of shortcuts. "Don't go looking for it, though. You can't find it. It finds you. That's the rule." She paused with her hand on the screen. "Well. That's what Gran says. Take it with a grain of salt. She also says the moon is a button."

And she was gone, out the back door and down the lane, and Pip was left holding a duck sock that smelled like toast, in a house where every room was a question.

Ollie tugged his sleeve. "Pip."

"Yeah?"

"Can I keep the duck sock?"

Pip looked at his little brother, who had lost nothing yet, who still had Captain Chomp with both mismatched buttons and a tail sewn twice, who believed a dinosaur could be a secret pirate and that the moon might, reasonably, be a button.

"Yeah," said Pip. "You can keep the duck sock."

Ollie hugged it like a treasure. Pip went to the window and looked down the hill at the school, at the bin, at the red mitten still waving in the last of the light. He had the feeling again, the one from the driveway - that the town was full of things set down and never picked up, and that somewhere, quietly, somebody was keeping track.

He didn't know how right he was. He didn't know that in one day Captain Chomp would be gone, and that the duck sock would matter more than anything he could imagine, and that a small green door was waiting behind a lost-and-found bin for someone who had lost something he truly loved.

He only knew that the dryer had eaten a sock, and left a stranger, and that Marrow's End was not, it turned out, a town that held still.

"Pip," said Ollie from the folding table, very seriously. "If Captain Chomp ever got lost, would you find him?"

Pip turned around. "Yes," he said. "I'd label him first. Then I'd find him."

Ollie nodded, satisfied, and went to put the duck sock in his drawer with his other treasures. And Pip, who was eleven and had decided things, decided one more thing before bed: that he would never, ever let anything in this family get lost.

The dryer hummed. Somewhere under the hill, very faintly, something hummed back.

## Chapter 3 - The Dinosaur Who Vanished Mid-Roar

Ollie woke up roaring, which was normal, and stopped roaring, which was not.

Pip heard it from his room - the roar cut off like a radio unplugged - and then a silence so complete it had edges. He was out of bed and down the hall before he'd decided to be, his bare feet slapping the cold floor, past the label that said STAIRS, WATCH THE MIDDLE ONE, into Ollie's room.

Ollie was sitting up in bed. His hands were open on the blanket in front of him, palms up, empty. He was looking at them like they had betrayed him.

"Chomp," he said.

Pip looked at the bed. The blanket was rumpled into the shape of a small boy and, beside it, the shape of nothing. There was the dent where a head had been. There was the grape-juice smell, faint, fading. There was no dinosaur.

"He was right here," said Ollie. His voice was doing the small-and-loud thing, climbing. "He was right here, Pip. I put him under my chin. I always put him under my chin. He doesn't get up in the night. He's not a real pirate, he's a secret pirate, he stays-"

"Okay," said Pip. "Okay. We'll find him."

He said it the way you say a thing to make it true. He was eleven. He had a label maker. He had decided, just last night, that nothing in this family would ever get lost. The universe, it seemed, had heard him and taken it as a challenge.

They looked under the bed. They looked in the toy bin, and in the closet, and behind the curtain, and in the one drawer Ollie kept his treasures in, where the duck sock lay folded like a small gray flag. They looked in the hallway and on the stairs, watching the middle one. Pip's mother helped, and Pip's father helped, and they moved the couch cushions and checked the laundry and looked in the refrigerator, which Pip's father did without explaining, because at a certain point in a search you stop explaining.

Captain Chomp was not in the house.

Pip, because he was Pip, had started labeling the places they'd searched. UNDER BED: CHECKED. TOY BIN: CHECKED. FRIDGE: CHECKED, DAD'S IDEA, DON'T ASK. He stuck them up as they went, a trail of little white flags, so they wouldn't look in the same place twice. It was the only thing he could think of to do with his hands that felt like doing something. Ollie watched him and then, solemnly, took the label maker and printed one more, all by himself, sounding out the letters. He pressed it onto his own chest.

OLLIE," it said. "NOT LOST."

Pip had to look away for a second so his eyes could do a thing he didn't want them to do in front of his brother.

Pip stood in the middle of Ollie's room and felt the floor tilt a little. It was a stuffed dinosaur. It was a green, one-loose-button, twice-sewn-tail, grape-juice-smelling stuffed dinosaur, and it was not in the house, and Ollie was making a sound now that wasn't quite crying and wasn't quite roaring, a sound Pip had never heard his brother make and never wanted to hear again.

"I'll find him," Pip said.

"You can't," said Ollie. "He's gone. He's lost." And then, in a whisper that was somehow louder than the roaring, "What if he doesn't know where home is?"

Pip thought of the label he hadn't been allowed to print. *He already knows where home is. He doesn't need it written down.* He had hoped that was true. He had hoped it so hard.

"He knows," said Pip, and he made his voice be a thing you could stand on. "He knows. And I know. So between the two of us, that's plenty."

Ollie looked at him. His face was wet and furious and six. "I'm coming," he said.

"Ollie-"

"I'm coming. He's my captain." Ollie climbed out of bed and put on his shoes, on the wrong feet, and did not fix them, because there was no time and also because it was a statement. "I'm brave. I'm very brave. I'm the bravest."

"I know," said Pip, and he did know, because Ollie was terrified and putting on his shoes anyway, and that, Pip thought, was what brave actually was, even if Ollie would have argued about the definition at full volume.

They told their mother they were going to look around the neighborhood. Their mother, who had the tired-and-proud look, said to stay where she could see them, and to be back for lunch, and she kissed Ollie's hair, which smelled like sleep and like the place where a dinosaur had been.

They went down the hill. Marrow's End in the morning was a different town than Marrow's End at dusk - brighter, busier, less like a folded sweater and more like a sweater being worn. A dog barked. A screen door slapped. The bakery smelled like bread and something sweeter underneath, and Pip, passing it, noticed for the first time a dog bowl by the door, a blue ceramic bowl with a chip in the rim, full of clean water. He didn't know why he noticed it. He noticed it anyway.

They checked the sidewalk. They checked the park, where a swing moved by itself in the wind like it was thinking. They checked the little library box on the post, and the bench by the war memorial, and the gap in the hedge where a cat lived, and the cat looked at them with the flat, unimpressed face of a creature who had never lost anything in its life and did not intend to start.

No Captain Chomp.

Ollie's hand found Pip's. It was small and hot and gripping hard. "Pip," he said. "What if someone took him?"

"Nobody took him," said Pip, too fast.

"What if he's lost-lost?" Ollie's voice cracked on it. "What if he's so lost he forgets us?"

Pip stopped walking. He crouched down so he was looking at Ollie's wrong-footed shoes and then up at Ollie's face. "Listen to me," he said. "Captain Chomp is not lost-lost. He's lost-found. There's a difference. Lost-found means he's somewhere, waiting, and we haven't gotten to him yet. That's all it means."

"How do you know?"

Because I have a label maker, Pip almost said, and heard how stupid it would sound. Instead he said, "Because I'm not going to stop. That's how I know."

Ollie nodded. He wiped his face with the back of his hand, which made it worse, and then he did the bravest thing Pip had ever seen him do, which was to keep walking.

They came to the school. The brick wall, the gate, the wooden bin. LOST AND FOUND. It was still overflowing, still waving its one red mitten. Pip had walked past it a dozen times in his head since yesterday, and now he stood in front of it, and something in him went quiet and certain, the way a compass goes quiet and certain when it finally finds north.

"He'd be here," Pip said. "If anyone found him, they'd put him here. That's what this is for."

He reached into the bin. He moved the mitten. He moved a water bottle, and a single glove, and a scarf, and a toy car with one wheel, and a book about trucks, and a hat that was too small for anyone. He moved them gently, because each one was somebody's, each one was waiting. Ollie stood on his toes and peered in, breathing hard.

At the bottom of the bin there was nothing.

Pip's hand went still. Ollie made the sound again, the small one.

And then Pip saw it.

Behind the bin, half hidden by it, set into the brick wall of the school where there had never, in any version of this town that Pip had seen, been a door - there was a door. It was small, and it was green, the green of a thing that has been painted and repainted and loved until the color gives up. It had a brass knob. It had a little brass plate. And it was, Pip realized with a lurch that went from his stomach to his shoes, exactly the green of Captain Chomp.

He hadn't seen it yesterday. He was sure he hadn't seen it yesterday. He had looked right at this wall.

"Pip," said Ollie, tugging. "Pip, what's that?"

Pip didn't answer. He was reading the little brass plate, and the letters on it were engraved, and they said:

BUREAU OF LOST THINGS.
PLEASE KNOCK.

And from behind the small green door, very faintly, came a sound like a humming, and a sound like a walrus, very politely, clearing its throat.

## Chapter 4 - Please Knock (The Door Is Shy)

Pip knocked.

He knocked the way you knock on a door you're not sure is real - two soft taps, then a pause, then one more, apologetic. Ollie stood beside him with his shoes on the wrong feet and his OLLIE, NOT LOST label on his chest, and he did not hide behind Pip, which took an amount of courage Pip promised himself he would remember later.

The brass knob turned by itself. The small green door swung inward. And the wall of the school, which should have been behind it, was not.

Behind the door was a room. Behind the room was another room. Behind that, rooms Pip's eyes couldn't finish, going back and back and up, shelves rising like the ribs of some enormous, gentle animal, ladders on wheels, lamps with green shades, and a smell - dust and lavender and old paper and, underneath, the grape-juice-and-sleep smell of a thing that has been loved. The ceiling was so far away it had weather. Somewhere in the distance a clock ticked with the patience of a thing that had all the time there was.

"Oh," said Ollie, very quietly, for him.

A walrus was standing at a desk just inside the door.

Pip knew it was a walrus because it was large and gray and had tusks and whiskers and flippers, and because it was wearing a waistcoat. The waistcoat was brown and had pockets, and the pockets had pens in them, and one of the pens was leaking. The walrus was dabbing at the leak with a handkerchief, and he looked up, and his eyes were small and dark and kind, the kindest eyes Pip had ever seen on a face that large.

"Ah," said the walrus. His voice was deep and a little wet, like a cello played in a bathtub. "Visitors. And one of them is six." He looked at Ollie. "I can tell. The shoes."

Ollie looked down at his shoes. "They're brave shoes," he said.

"They are very brave shoes," the walrus agreed, without a flicker of mockery. "I am Barnaby. I am the Chief of the Bureau of Lost Things. You have lost something." He said it not as a question but as a gentle fact, the way a doctor says *you have a heartbeat*. "That is why you can see the door. The door is shy. It only shows itself to people who have lost something they truly love."

Pip found his voice. "My brother's dinosaur. Captain Chomp. He's-" Pip's throat did a thing. "He's a secret pirate. He was under Ollie's chin and then he wasn't. We looked everywhere. We labeled everywhere."

Barnaby nodded slowly, and his whiskers moved like a field of wheat in a small wind. "A dinosaur. Green. One loose button eye. A tail that has been sewn twice." He said it reading from nothing, from the air, from the ledger of the world. "Yes. He is here. He was brought in this morning. He is safe. He is being dusted."

Ollie made a sound like a balloon letting out a little of its air. "Can I-"

"In a moment," said Barnaby, gently. "There is a form."

"There's a form?" said Pip.

"There is always a form," said Barnaby, with the weary pride of a creature who has made peace with forms. "But come in, come in. You are letting the outside in, and the outside is very drafty." He heaved himself around the desk, and his flippers made a sound on the floor like two wet hands applauding, and he gestured with a pen that was still leaking. "Welcome to the Bureau. Mind the step. The step is also lost. We are keeping it for someone."

Pip stepped over a small wooden step that sat in the middle of the floor for no reason, labeled, in a handwriting that was not his, STEP. STILL LOOKING. And then he was inside, and the door swung shut behind him, and the sound of the schoolyard - the dog, the screen door, the wind in the swing - went out like a candle.

It was quiet in the Bureau. Not empty-quiet. Full-quiet. The quiet of a library, of a museum after hours, of a house where everyone you love is asleep. The shelves went up and up, and on them, in rows, in bins, in jars, in little labeled drawers, were the lost things of Marrow's End.

Pip walked forward like a boy in a dream. There was a shelf of single gloves, each one pinned with a note. There was a jar of keys, hundreds of keys, and the jar hummed faintly when you passed it, like the keys were remembering their locks. There was a rack of umbrellas, dozens of them, furled and patient, and a sign that said UMBRELLA ORCHARD. PLEASE DO NOT WATER. There was a drawer labeled HOMEWORK EXCUSES, VALID, and another labeled HOMEWORK EXCUSES, DUBIOUS. There was a whole wall of buttons, sorted by color, and a whole wall of marbles, sorted by nothing Pip could understand, and a bin of spectacle frames with no lenses, and a bin of lenses with no frames, and the two bins sat next to each other like they were hoping.

And there were socks.

There were socks the way the sea has water. Shelves of them, bins of them, a whole wing of them, single socks, lonely socks, each one tagged and folded and waiting. The smell of them was enormous and various - toast and rain and grass and someone else's house - and Pip thought of the duck sock in Ollie's drawer and felt the whole thing click into place, the dryers, the trades, the front door.

"The dryers," he said.

"Ah," said Barnaby, following his gaze. "Yes. The dryers are one of our intake points. We have several. The dryers, the gaps behind things, the spaces between couch cushions, the bottoms of bags, the moments when you are sure, absolutely sure, you just had it." He sighed, a long, soggy, contented sigh. "Everything that is lost comes here, eventually. We file it. We dust it. We wait with it."

"We wait with it?" said Pip.

"Waiting is the important part," said Barnaby. "A thing that is lost is not gone. It is only waiting. And a thing that is waiting should not wait alone." He looked at Pip with those small, kind eyes. "That is the whole of the Bureau, really. The rest is paperwork."

As if summoned by the word, a cat walked past carrying a ledger almost as big as she was. She was gray and precise, with a white shirt-front of fur and a pair of spectacles perched on her nose that she did not appear to need but wore anyway, for the authority. She glanced at Pip, then at Ollie, then at Barnaby's leaking pen.

"You're dripping on the intake log," she said. "Again."

"Miss Whisk," said Barnaby, with enormous dignity, "is our Head Cataloguer. She keeps the record of every thing that comes in and every thing that goes out."

"And every thing that doesn't," said Miss Whisk, without stopping. "Which lately is a longer list than it ought to be." She said it lightly, but the lightness had a floor under it, and Pip noticed, and filed it. She vanished between two shelves, and the ledger did not wobble, and her tail said, quite clearly, *don't follow me.*

"She is fond of you already," Barnaby whispered. "She only complains about people she intends to keep."

Ollie had wandered to a low shelf and was looking at a row of stuffed animals, each one tagged, each one dusted, each one facing out, patient. He did not touch them. He just looked, and his face did something complicated.

"Pip," he said. "They're all waiting for somebody."

"Yes," said Barnaby, softly. "Every one."

And Pip, who was eleven, who had a label maker, who had decided that nothing in his family would ever get lost, stood in the middle of the Bureau of Lost Things and understood, for the first time, that lost was not the same as gone, and that somewhere in this enormous, kindly, slightly chaotic room, Captain Chomp was waiting, and being dusted, and not waiting alone.

He also understood, a beat later, that he was going to cry, and that he was not going to, because Ollie was watching, and because there was a form.

"Right," said Pip, and his voice only wobbled a little. "The form."

Barnaby beamed, which on a walrus is a large and damp event. "Splendid," he said. "I do love a boy who accepts the form."

## Chapter 5 - The Pocket of Useless Junk

The form was four pages long and asked, among other things, for the dinosaur's name, rank, and distinguishing marks, and Pip was filling in *secret pirate* under OCCUPATION when the door opened again and Goldie Finch came in, eating an apple.

"Hey," she said. "You found it. I figured you would. You've got the look."

Pip stared at her. "You can see the door?"

"Obviously I can see the door." Goldie took a bite of apple. "I've been coming here since I was seven. Gran used to bring me. She'd lose a button on purpose just to get the door to show, and then she'd say hello to Barnaby and have a biscuit and go home. It was her whole social life." She nodded at the walrus. "Hi, Barnaby."

"Miss Finch," said Barnaby, warmly, leaking. "A pleasure, as always. You are just in time for the form."

"I'm never just in time for the form," said Goldie. "I'm allergic to the form. I'll be over here." She wandered off toward the shelves with the easy, shortcutting walk of a girl who knew every aisle, and Pip, abandoning OCCUPATION, went after her, because he had a question that had been growing in him since the driveway.

"How?" he said. "The door. It only shows if you've lost something you truly love. But you-" He gestured at her pocket, which jingled. "You lose things all the time. You don't even mind. You lost your good acorn yesterday and you just shrugged."

Goldie stopped at a shelf of single mittens and looked at him. For a second the easy, shortcutting look went off her face, and underneath it was something older and quieter.

"I love my stuff," she said. "I just don't mind when it goes. Those are two different things, Pip. People think they're the same thing. They're not."

She put the apple core in her pocket - Pip watched her do it, watched the pocket accept it without complaint - and then she turned the pocket half inside out, the way she had at the house, and let the contents spill into her palm.

"Exhibit A," she said. "The pocket of useless junk."

There, in her hand, were three things. A marble, a paperclip, and a rubber band.

The marble was glass, a cat's-eye, a swirl of green and gold trapped inside it like a small frozen weather. The paperclip was silver and bent, bent the way a paperclip gets when it has been picked at and twisted and thought with. The rubber band was red, and a little cracked, and stretched out of shape from a life of holding things together that were not its job to hold.

"This," said Goldie, holding up the marble, "is a marble. It's useless. You can't eat it, you can't wear it, it's no good for anything except rolling, and it's always rolling under things. I've had it since I was six. I love it." She set it on the shelf, and it rolled an inch, and she stopped it with one finger without looking. "This," holding up the paperclip, "is a paperclip. It's bent. It's useless. It can't hold paper anymore, not really. I love it." She hooked it onto her collar. "And this," holding up the rubber band, "is a rubber band. It's cracked. It's useless. It's about to snap, honestly, any day now. I love it."

Pip looked at the three useless things. "Why do you love them if they're useless?"

Goldie looked at him like he'd asked why the sky was up. "Because they're mine," she said. "Because I've had them. Because the marble's got a chip on it from the time I dropped it on the church steps, and the paperclip's bent because I bent it thinking about my dad, and the rubber band held my hair the day I learned to ride a bike. Useless isn't the opposite of loved, Pip. Useless is just what a thing is after it's done being useful. The loving's still in there."

She put the marble back in the pocket, and the paperclip, and the rubber band, and the pocket jingled its small, satisfied jingle, and Pip felt something in his chest rearrange itself, quietly, like furniture being moved in a room he hadn't known he had.

"You're going to lose them someday," he said. It wasn't a question. He was eleven. He had decided things. He knew how this went.

"Probably," said Goldie, and she smiled, and the smile was the calmest thing Pip had ever seen. "The marble'll roll under something I can't reach. The paperclip'll fall out of a hole in this pocket. The rubber band'll snap." She patted the pocket. "And I'll be sad. And then I'll remember the church steps, and your dad, and the bike. And the sad'll be the good kind. The kind that means it was real."

Pip thought of Captain Chomp, being dusted somewhere in this enormous room. He thought of Ollie, over by the low shelf, not touching the waiting animals. He thought of a house with too many curtains, holding its breath, keeping everything, never letting anything go, never letting anything be the good kind of sad.

"I don't think I can do that," he said honestly. "The not-minding part."

"You don't have to," said Goldie. "Not yet. Maybe not ever. That's okay too." She hooked her arm through his, which was a very Goldie thing to do, a shortcut through a conversation. "Come on. I'll show you the good shelves. Barnaby'll do the form till the cows come home, and the cows, by the way, are also lost, we have a whole field of them, don't ask."

She led him down an aisle, and then another, taking turns Pip was sure didn't exist, past the umbrella orchard and the jar of humming keys, past a shelf of single earrings that glittered like a city at night, past a bin labeled LEFT SHOES, ALL OF THEM, WHY.

In a low, warm corner they came to a hedgehog. He was small and round and the color of toast, and he was standing on a stool in front of a shelf of single socks, holding one up to his nose with the gravity of a scientist holding a beaker. His quills were full of buttons - dozens of them, stuck in among the spines like fruit in a very prickly bush - and they clicked softly when he moved.

"That's Thimble," Goldie whispered. "He sorts the socks by smell. Don't interrupt him. He's doing the toast ones."

The hedgehog sniffed the sock, closed his eyes, and placed it gently into a bin labeled WARM / BREAKFAST / SOMEONE'S KITCHEN. Then he picked up another, sniffed, and frowned. "Rain," he murmured. "And dog. And a little bit of sadness. That's a difficult one." He set it aside in a bin labeled COMPLICATED, and only then did he notice them, and give a small, formal nod, and go back to his smelling.

"He's been here longer than anyone," Goldie said, as they moved on. "He says every sock smells like the person who lost it. He says if you smell hard enough you can find your way home." She said it lightly, but Pip filed it, the way he filed everything, in a drawer he was starting to suspect he would need.

And as they walked, Goldie talked, and the Bureau unfolded around them, and Pip - who had come here to find a dinosaur - began, slowly, to find something else, something he didn't have a label for yet, something about the difference between a thing that is lost and a thing that is gone, and the difference between loving a thing and keeping it, and how the pocket of a girl who didn't mind could hold more than a house with too many curtains.

"We'll get him back," Goldie said, quietly, like she'd been reading the back of his head. "Captain Chomp. That's what this place is for. Things come home here. That's the whole point of the whole place." She squeezed his arm once, let go, and pointed her apple-less hand down the aisle like a captain. "Now come on. Barnaby's about to explain Finding Day, and he uses props."

Behind them, at the desk, Barnaby stamped the form with a flipper, and the stamp said RECEIVED, and the ink was a little smudged, because the pen was still leaking, and because some things, in the Bureau of Lost Things, are allowed to be a little smudged and still be true.

## Chapter 6 - Form 27-B: Missing, Possibly Forever

Barnaby did, in fact, use props.

He produced, from somewhere under the desk, a snow globe. Inside the snow globe was a tiny green door. He set it on the desk between them with the ceremony of a magician producing a dove, and then he produced a second prop, which was a biscuit, which he ate, because explaining made him peckish.

"Right," he said, crumbs on his whiskers. "The rules. There are three. I will say them once, and then I will say them again, because I am a walrus and we are thorough."

Pip sat up straight. Ollie sat up straighter. Goldie leaned against the desk, already bored, already knowing this part.

"Rule one," said Barnaby, holding up a flipper. "You can only see the door if you have lost something you truly love. Not something you merely like. Not something you are merely inconvenienced by. Something you love. The door knows the difference. The door is very good at its job." He tapped the snow globe. "You three have all lost something you love. That is why you are here. That is why the door showed itself. It is not a magic trick. It is a sympathy."

"Rule two," he continued, holding up the other flipper, which still had a little biscuit on it. "A thing that is lost is not gone. It is waiting. We keep it safe. We dust it. We help it remember." He leaned forward, and his voice went softer, and the whole enormous room seemed to lean in with him. "Because here is the thing about being lost, and it is the hard thing, so I will say it gently. When a thing is lost, it starts to forget. It forgets the way home. And if it stays lost too long - if it is not claimed, if it is not loved back - then the person who lost it starts to forget too. Not the thing. The *loving*. They forget how much they loved it. And then the thing is not lost anymore. It is just... gone."

The room was very quiet. Ollie's hand found Pip's again.

"That's why we wait with them," said Barnaby. "So they don't forget. So the loving stays warm. A thing that is waited with remembers. A thing that is loved back finds its way." He looked at Ollie. "Captain Chomp is being waited with. He is being loved back. He has not forgotten you. He roars in his sleep. The staff find it very comforting."

Ollie's face did the complicated thing again, but this time it landed somewhere warm. "He does?" he whispered.

"He does," said Barnaby. "A secret pirate's roar. Very fierce. Very small."

"Rule three," said Barnaby, and he set down the snow globe and folded his flippers, and for the first time he looked not just kind but serious, in the way that kind people look when they are about to tell you the true thing. "Once a year, on the first day of fall, we open the big door. Not the little green one you came through. The big one. The one at the back that none of you have seen yet. And on that day, every lost thing that is ready - every thing that has been claimed and loved back and remembers the way - gets to go home. All at once. One day. We call it Finding Day."

"When is it?" said Pip.

Barnaby produced a third prop, which was a calendar, and it was a calendar of autumn leaves, and he turned it to a page with a single leaf circled in red.

"Six days from today," he said. "The first day of fall. Finding Day. It is the one day the way home is open and clear. The rest of the year the way is... difficult. Twisted. A lost thing can wander for months and not find it. But on Finding Day, the door is wide, and the road is straight, and everything that is ready goes home." He looked at Pip. "Captain Chomp was very lost when he came in. Frightened. He has forgotten a good deal of the way. He needs the week to remember. And he needs to be claimed, and loved back, and helped. That is your job, if you want it."

"I want it," said Pip, so fast it surprised him.

"I want it too," said Ollie, at full volume, which was his way of signing a contract.

"Then you will come," said Barnaby. "Every day, if you like. You will sit with him. You will tell him about home. You will remind him of the grape juice and the chin and the roaring. You will love him back. And on Finding Day, if he is ready, he will go home with you." He paused, and his small eyes were very gentle. "Most things are ready. Most things go home. It is a good day. The best day."

Pip heard the *most*. He was eleven. He heard the word that was doing the quiet work in that sentence, the word that meant *not all*, the word that meant there was a reason Barnaby had said rule two so gently. He thought of Miss Whisk's *and every thing that doesn't, which lately is a longer list than it ought to be.* He filed it, and he did not say it out loud, because Ollie was signing contracts at full volume and did not need the *most* right now.

"What happens," Pip asked carefully, "to the things that aren't ready?"

Barnaby was quiet for a moment. "They wait another year," he said. "We keep them safe. We keep loving them back. And we hope." He did not say the rest, the *and if they wait too long the loving goes out*, because he had already said it gently once and did not believe in saying hard things twice to a six-year-old. But Pip heard it anyway, in the shape of the silence.

"Okay," said Pip. "Then we'll make sure he's ready."

Barnaby beamed the large, damp beaming. "Splendid. Now. The rest of the form." He pushed the four pages toward Pip. "Sign here. And here. And initial the dinosaur's rank. And I will need a description of the home, for the file. The more love in the description, the better. The way home is paved with the details."

So Pip wrote. He wrote about the narrow house and the creaky middle stair and the refrigerator that hummed like it knew a secret. He wrote about the pizza on the floor and the box labeled TABLE, LEGS, PROBABLY. He wrote about Ollie's wrong-footed brave shoes and the duck sock that smelled like toast and the grape-juice smell and the chin and the roaring. He wrote until the description of home was a page long, and then, because Barnaby said the more love the better, he wrote a little more.

When he was done, Barnaby stamped it, and the stamp said RECEIVED, and he filed it in a drawer that was exactly the size of the paper, because the Bureau was like that.

"Welcome to the Bureau, Pip Parker," said Barnaby. "You are now a Friend of the Lost. It is not an official title. I just made it up. But it is true."

"He makes up a lot of titles," Goldie said. "I'm a Knight of the Shortcut. It's not official either. There's no sword. I asked."

"There is a sword," said Barnaby, with dignity. "It is lost. We are keeping it for you."

Pip looked at the stamp, and at the snow globe with the tiny green door, and at Ollie, who was already planning his first visit out loud, at full volume, to a dinosaur who was somewhere in this enormous, kindly room, roaring in his sleep, being waited with, not forgotten.

One week. Finding Day. They had one week to love a dinosaur back to remembering.

Pip decided, then and there, that he was going to be the best Friend of the Lost this Bureau had ever seen. He did not know, yet, that something in the Bureau was taking things in the night, and that the list of the not-ready was growing, and that a lonely man on a hill was building a machine so that nothing he loved would ever be lost again.

He only knew that he had a week, and a form, and a brother, and a small green dinosaur who roared in his sleep.

It was, he thought, enough to start with.

## Chapter 7 - The Very Serious Science of Left Socks

On the third day, Pip and Ollie came to the Bureau to love Captain Chomp back to remembering, and instead they got recruited by a hedgehog.

It happened like this. They had found Captain Chomp on a low shelf in a section labeled SOFT / BRAVE / PRETEND, and Ollie was sitting cross-legged in front of him, telling him about the house in great detail, at a volume that was respectful for Ollie, which is to say only slightly louder than a normal person's shout. Captain Chomp sat with his two mismatched buttons and his twice-sewn tail, and he was, Pip noticed, a little faded, a little unsure, the way a thing gets when it has been lost. But when Ollie said *and the middle stair goes SKREEE*, one of Captain Chomp's buttons seemed to catch the light, just a little, like an eye remembering.

"It's working," Pip whispered.

"Of course it's working," said Ollie. "I'm very good at remembering."

And that was when the hedgehog appeared, pushing a little cart of single socks, and stopped, and looked at them, and said, "You. You have a nose."

Pip touched his nose. "I... do?"

"Everyone does. Most people don't use it." The hedgehog set down the cart. He was small and round and the color of toast, and his quills were full of buttons, and he had the air of someone who had been interrupted in the middle of something important and had decided to make it your problem. "I am Thimble. I sort the socks. You are the new Friends of the Lost. Barnaby told me. He makes up titles. It's charming. Come with me. I need a second nose."

"I'm six," said Ollie.

"Excellent," said Thimble. "Six-year-old noses are the most honest. They haven't learned to be polite yet. Come."

And because you did not argue with Thimble - Pip would learn this was a law of the Bureau, like gravity, or forms - they went.

The sock wing was enormous. It went on and on, shelves and bins and drawers of single socks, each one tagged, each one folded, each one waiting. And Thimble walked them down it the way a general walks a battlefield, or a conductor walks an orchestra, with a small baton that was actually a knitting needle.

"The sock," said Thimble, "is the most honest thing a person owns. A shoe lies. A shoe says *I am a sensible shoe* when really it stepped in something. A hat lies. A hat says *I am a hat* when really it is hiding a bad haircut. But a sock cannot lie. A sock has been against the skin all day. A sock knows." He stopped at a bin and held up a small gray sock. "Smell."

Pip smelled. "Toast," he said.

"Toast," Thimble agreed, pleased. "And underneath?"

Pip smelled again. "Someone's kitchen. A warm one. There's a... a radio?"

"A radio," said Thimble, and he closed his eyes with the bliss of a scholar whose theory has been confirmed. "You see. This sock belongs to someone who makes toast in a warm kitchen with the radio on. That is a whole life. That is a whole person. And the sock remembers it, even when the person has forgotten the sock." He placed it gently in a bin labeled WARM / BREAKFAST / RADIO. "This is the taxonomy. We sort by smell. It is the only honest way."

He led them on, and the taxonomy unfolded, and it was, Pip realized, completely insane and completely perfect.

There was a bin labeled GRASS / SATURDAY / SOMEONE'S BEST DAY, full of socks that smelled of cut grass and sunshine. There was a bin labeled RAIN / BUS STOP / PATIENCE. There was a bin labeled DOG / MUD / NO REGRETS. There was a bin labeled HOSPITAL / WAITING / BRAVE, and Thimble touched that one gently and did not make a joke, and Pip understood that the taxonomy was not just a system, it was a kindness, a way of saying *your life was here, it mattered, we noticed.*

And there was a bin labeled COMPLICATED.

"The complicated ones," said Thimble, "smell like more than one thing. Like a person who is going through something. We keep those separate. We give them extra dusting." He picked one up, sniffed, and frowned. "Rain. And dog. And a little bit of sadness. This one's been here a while." He set it back. "We wait with the complicated ones the longest."

Ollie, who had been listening with his whole face, said, "Do you ever get them wrong?"

Thimble stopped. He looked at Ollie for a long moment. Then he said, with enormous seriousness, "Yes. Sometimes a sock smells like toast but it is actually a sock that belongs to someone who is very sad and makes toast to feel better. So I put it in WARM / BREAKFAST, but I also put a little star on the tag. The star means *be gentle with this one.*" He looked at Ollie. "The system is not perfect. The system is a guess. But it is a guess that pays attention. That is the best a system can be."

Pip felt something go warm in his chest. He thought of his label maker, of STAIRS, WATCH THE MIDDLE ONE, of FRIDGE, COLD, MOSTLY. He had always thought labels were for knowing. Thimble's tags were for noticing. They were not the same thing. He was starting to learn they were not the same thing at all.

It was then that Pip noticed the buttons.

They were everywhere in Thimble's quills. Dozens of them. Buttons of every color and size, stuck in among the spines like fruit in a very prickly bush, clicking softly when he moved. A blue one. A brass one. A little pearl one. A red one shaped like a star.

"You have buttons in your quills," said Pip.

"I do," said Thimble.

"Why?"

Thimble looked at him, and then he did something Pip did not expect. He reached up, very carefully, and worked a button loose from his quills - a small brown one, four-holed - and he held it out in his palm.

"Because every coat loses one," he said. "Eventually. Every coat, every shirt, every dinosaur." He glanced at Ollie, and at Captain Chomp, who was back on his shelf, listening. "A button is the thing that holds a thing together. And buttons come loose. They always come loose. So I keep spares. When a thing comes in here with a button missing, or a button loose, I give it one of mine. I sew it on. I make it ready to go home." He closed his palm around the brown button. "I have been doing this a long time. I have a great many spares. I am, you might say, prepared for loss."

He said it lightly, but Pip heard the floor under the lightness, the way he'd heard it under Miss Whisk. *Prepared for loss.* A hedgehog who kept spare buttons because he knew, better than anyone, that things come loose, that things come apart, that the holding-together is the fragile part.

"Can I have one?" said Ollie. "For Captain Chomp? His eye's loose."

Thimble looked at Ollie. Then he reached up and worked loose a button - a green one, round, four-holed, almost exactly the green of Captain Chomp. He held it out.

"For the captain," he said. "A spare. In case. That is what spares are for."

Ollie took it with both hands, like it was made of glass, like it was made of gold. "Thank you," he said, and it came out so sincere and so loud that several shelves of socks seemed to lean in.

Thimble looked embarrassed, which on a hedgehog is a small, prickly event. "Yes. Well. Go on. Love your dinosaur back. I have the rain ones to do. The rain ones are difficult. They smell like bus stops." He picked up his cart. And then, over his shoulder, so quietly Pip almost missed it: "The spare is a promise, you know. It says *I expect you to come loose, and I expect to fix you, and I am not going anywhere.* That is what a spare button says."

He trundled off down the sock wing, and his quills clicked, and Pip stood there holding the shape of that sentence in his head, turning it over, filing it in the drawer he was building for things that mattered.

Ollie was already running back to Captain Chomp, the green button clutched in his fist, ready to tell a dinosaur about a spare, about a promise, about a hedgehog who was prepared for loss and was not going anywhere.

Pip followed. And as he went, he glanced back once at the sock wing, at the bins and the tags and the little stars that meant *be gentle with this one*, and he thought that if being lost was a thing that happened to everyone, then being noticed - being smelled and sorted and waited with - was the thing that made it bearable.

He did not notice, then, the small thing that would matter later. He did not notice that one of the bins in the sock wing - a bin labeled WARM / BREAKFAST / RADIO - was emptier than it had been yesterday. He did not notice that a single gray duck sock, which Ollie had brought from home to show Captain Chomp, was gone from the shelf where he'd set it down.

He only noticed, later, that the humming had started. But that was later. For now, there was a dinosaur to love back, and a spare button, and a promise.

For now, that was enough.

## Chapter 8 - The Cat Who Kept the Record

Miss Whisk did not like visitors, and she did not like interruptions, and she did not like the way Barnaby leaked on the intake log, and she made all three of these things clear by the simple method of continuing to work while Pip and Ollie stood in front of her desk, waiting.

Her desk was the tidiest thing in the Bureau. Everything on it had a place, and everything was in its place, and the places were labeled in a handwriting so precise it looked printed. IN. OUT. PENDING. DO NOT. There was a stamp for each, and Miss Whisk used them the way a pianist uses keys, without looking, with a kind of furious grace.

"What's the DO NOT stamp for?" Ollie asked, pointing.

"Things that must not," said Miss Whisk.

"Like what?"

"Like being touched by six-year-olds," said Miss Whisk, and stamped the air near Ollie's hand, DO NOT, and Ollie giggled, which was the first giggle anyone had gotten out of her all week, and she pretended it had not happened, which was also a kind of grace.

"You're the dinosaur people," she said, not looking up.

"Captain Chomp," said Ollie. "He's a secret pirate."

"He's a dinosaur who was lost and is now found and is being loved back, which is more than most things get, so he should count himself lucky." Miss Whisk stamped something IN. "I'm the Head Cataloguer. I keep the record. Every thing that comes in, every thing that goes out. If it's not in the record, it didn't happen. If it is in the record, it happened whether you like it or not. The record is the truth. I am the truth's secretary. It's a living."

Pip, who loved records, who loved labels, who loved the idea that a thing could be written down and therefore held, felt an immediate and embarrassing kinship with this cat. "Can I see it?" he asked. "The record?"

Miss Whisk looked up for the first time. She looked at Pip the way you look at a dog that has offered to do your taxes. "You want to see the record."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because," said Pip, and he thought about it, because it was a real question, "because if it's written down, it can't get lost. The record keeps the things. Even if the things go somewhere, the record still has them. That's... that's the whole point of writing things down. Isn't it?"

Miss Whisk stared at him for a long moment. Then she did something Pip would remember for the rest of his life, which was that the corner of her mouth moved, very slightly, in a direction that was almost a smile.

"Huh," she said. "The boy gets it." She heaved the great ledger around so it faced him. "Fine. Look. Don't touch. The record is not a pet."

The ledger was enormous. It was the size of a door, and it was full of entries, each one in Miss Whisk's precise hand. Pip read them, and they were not what he expected. He had expected a list of objects. What he got was a list of loves.

*One red mitten. Left hand. Knitted by a grandmother, since gone. The wool remembers her hands. Claimed by a girl who cries when she holds it. Status: waiting. Be gentle.*

*One toy car, one wheel. Belonged to a boy who is now taller. The boy does not want the car. The boy wants the being-small. We cannot file the being-small. We keep the car. Status: complicated.*

*One spectacle frame, no lens. Belonged to a man who read to his dog. The dog is gone. The man reads to the frame. Status: waiting. Extra dusting.*

Pip read, and read, and felt his chest do the thing it had been doing all week, the rearranging, the furniture-moving. The record was not a list of things. It was a list of what the things meant. Miss Whisk did not catalogue objects. She catalogued the loving.

"You write down the love," he said.

"I write down the relevant information," said Miss Whisk, but her ears did a small, pleased thing. "The love is relevant. A thing without its love is just inventory. We are not a warehouse. We are a... " She paused, and for a second the precise, sarcastic surface went thin, and underneath it was something so tender it made Pip look away to give her privacy. "We are a waiting room," she said. "For the loving. The things are just the coats it wears."

Ollie, who had been quiet for an unusually long time, said, "Do you have a coat?"

Miss Whisk went very still.

"I beg your pardon," she said.

"A coat," said Ollie, undeterred, six and honest. "For your loving. Do you have a thing you're waiting for?"

The silence that followed had edges. Pip grabbed for Ollie's hand, but Ollie did not need grabbing; he stood there, small and loud and unafraid, asking the question nobody asked the cat, and Miss Whisk looked at him, and her tail went still, and her spectacles caught the lamplight so Pip couldn't see her eyes.

"I have a file," she said, at last. Her voice was flat. "Everyone has a file. Mine is in the back. It is not interesting. It is a bell." She turned the ledger back around, firmly, the audience over. "A bell. From a collar. It is lost. It has been lost a long time. I do not wait for it. I merely keep the file. There is a difference. The file is administrative."

But her paw, Pip noticed, had gone to her own collar - a plain leather collar with no bell on it - and touched it, once, the way you touch a place where something used to be. And then she stamped something PENDING with more force than was necessary, and said, "Go love your dinosaur. The record keeps itself. That's the point of the record."

Pip went. He pulled Ollie along. But at the end of the aisle he looked back, and Miss Whisk was not stamping. She was looking at the ledger, at one particular entry, and her paw was on her empty collar, and her face was the face of someone who kept the record of everyone's loving and had filed her own in the back, under *administrative*, where nobody would think to look.

Pip filed it. He filed it carefully, with a little star on the tag, the way Thimble did. *Be gentle with this one.*

They found Captain Chomp on his shelf, and Ollie told him about the cat and the bell and the record, and Captain Chomp's loose button caught the light. And it was then, as Pip reached to straighten the dinosaur's tag, that he saw the thing that would keep him awake that night.

On the shelf next to Captain Chomp, there was a tag. A neat little tag, in Miss Whisk's precise hand. It said:

*One music box. Plays a waltz. Belonged to a woman who danced in her kitchen. Claimed. Ready. Status: going home on Finding Day.*

But there was no music box.

There was a clean square in the dust, the exact shape of a music box, and a tag that said it was there, and nothing else. Pip looked at the clean square, and at the tag, and at the shelf, and he felt the floor tilt the way it had tilted in Ollie's room.

"Ollie," he said. "Where's the music box?"

Ollie looked. "There isn't one."

"The tag says there is."

"Then the tag's wrong," said Ollie, with the easy logic of six.

But Pip knew, with a certainty that went from his stomach to his shoes, that the tag was not wrong. Miss Whisk's tags were never wrong. The record was the truth. And the truth said there was a music box here, a music box that played a waltz, that belonged to a woman who danced in her kitchen, that was claimed and ready and going home on Finding Day.

And the truth was a clean square in the dust.

Pip looked down the long, quiet aisle, at the shelves and the bins and the waiting things, and for the first time the Bureau did not feel like a waiting room. It felt like a room where something had been, and was not, and had not left a note.

From somewhere far off, very faintly, came a humming.

## Chapter 9 - The Bowl for Stray Customers

On the third day, Goldie took them to the bakery by a shortcut that involved a fence, a gap in a hedge, and a cat who lived in the gap and who Goldie greeted by name, and Pip decided that Goldie did not walk through the town so much as the town agreed to let her through.

"Barnaby sent us," Goldie explained, as they emerged, smelling of hedge, into the alley behind the bakery. "The Bureau runs on buns. It's a whole thing. The Chief gets low if he doesn't have a bun by eleven. It's in his contract. He made up the contract. He's both parties."

The bakery was called The Warm Loaf, and it smelled like the inside of a good decision. The smell got into Pip's clothes and stayed there, and for the rest of his life, whenever he smelled bread, he would remember this day, though he did not know that yet.

Mrs. Crumb was behind the counter. She was a large, floury, unhurried woman with forearms like a rower and a face that had decided, a long time ago, to be kind and had never found a reason to stop. She looked at the three of them and she did not ask why they were there, because in Marrow's End you did not ask, you just added more buns.

"The usual for the Chief," she said, reaching for a bag. "And one for the road. And one for the small one, because the small one looks like he's been through something." She handed Ollie a bun shaped like a knot. Ollie accepted it with both hands and the gravity of a knight receiving a sword.

"Thank you," said Ollie. "I have been through something. My dinosaur's lost. But he's found. But he's not home yet. It's complicated."

"That's the worst kind," said Mrs. Crumb, without a flicker of mockery. "The found-but-not-home. I've had a few of those myself." She said it lightly, but Pip, who was learning to hear the floors under lightness, heard a floor. He filed it.

It was then that he noticed the bowl.

It was by the door, just inside, a blue ceramic bowl with a chip in the rim, full of clean water. It was not a decorative bowl. It was a working bowl, the kind you put down for a dog. And it was full, and the water was fresh, and there was a little mat under it, and the mat had a faded picture of a bone on it.

"You have a dog?" Pip asked.

Mrs. Crumb followed his gaze, and her face did something soft. "No," she said. "No dog of my own. But I keep the bowl. For stray customers."

"Stray customers?"

"Strays," said Mrs. Crumb. "The ones without a home. The ones who come in the night, or the early morning, when the shop's quiet. They don't come in for bread. They come in for the water, and the warm by the oven, and the being-noticed. I keep the bowl full. I keep the mat clean. And sometimes, in the morning, the bowl's empty, and there's a little mud by the door, and I know somebody came in out of the cold." She wiped her hands on her apron. "You don't have to own a thing to take care of it. You just have to leave the bowl out."

Pip looked at the blue bowl with the chipped rim, and he thought of the Bureau, of the waiting, of the *a thing that is waiting should not wait alone.* Mrs. Crumb did not know about the Bureau. Mrs. Crumb did not need to. Mrs. Crumb had figured out the whole thing on her own, with a bowl and a mat and an oven, and Pip felt a sudden, enormous respect for her that he did not have words for.

"Does the stray have a name?" Ollie asked.

Mrs. Crumb smiled. "I don't name them. Naming's a kind of keeping, and strays aren't for keeping. They're for feeding." She paused, and she looked out the window, toward the hill, toward the house with too many curtains. "Though there was one, once. Years ago. Belonged to the old inventor up the hill. Mr. Snell. Used to come in every morning, the dog did, with Mr. Snell, and sit by the oven while he bought his loaf. A good dog. A biscuit-colored dog, if you can believe that. Named Biscuit, because Mr. Snell said he was the color of a biscuit and also because he was, and I quote, 'the only thing in my life that's never once been late.'"

She said it fondly. But then her face did the thing, the soft-sad thing.

"That was eight years ago," she said. "The dog ran off. Mr. Snell looked for a while. Put up posters. The posters got rained on. He took them down. And he stopped coming in." She looked back at the bowl. "I kept the bowl out, though. After. In case. You keep the bowl out. That's the job."

Pip felt something click, quietly, in the drawer where he kept the things that mattered. The house with too many curtains. The man who stopped coming in. The dog that ran off eight years ago. The bowl that stayed full. He did not have the whole picture. He had a corner of it. But the corner was enough to make him look up the hill, through the bakery window, at the curtained house, holding its breath.

"Does he ever come back?" Ollie asked. "The dog?"

Mrs. Crumb was quiet for a moment. "I don't know," she said, honestly. "I like to think he's somewhere warm. I like to think somebody left a bowl out for him." She turned back to the counter, and her voice went bright again, the way kind people's voices do when they have decided not to be sad in front of children. "Now. Who wants a cinnamon knot? On the house. For the found-but-not-home. It's a difficult condition. It requires sugar."

They took the buns for the Chief, and the cinnamon knots, and Goldie took them back by a different shortcut, because Goldie never took the same shortcut twice, and Pip walked home with the smell of bread in his clothes and the blue bowl in his head.

He thought about Mrs. Crumb, who kept a bowl for a stray she did not own. He thought about Mr. Snell, who had stopped coming in. He thought about the difference between leaving a bowl out and closing all the curtains, and he could not quite say what the difference was, but he could feel it, the way you feel a step in the dark.

When they got to the Bureau, Barnaby ate his bun with the solemn joy of a walrus who has been low and is now restored, and he said, "Mrs. Crumb is a Friend of the Lost. She doesn't know it. I make up the titles. It's fine." And Pip, delivering the bun, said, "She keeps a bowl out. For strays."

Barnaby stopped chewing. He looked at Pip with his small, kind eyes, and for a moment he looked very old, and very tired, and very hopeful, all at once.

"Yes," he said, softly. "She does. She's been keeping that bowl out a long time." He finished his bun, and he brushed the crumbs from his waistcoat, and he said, in a voice that was almost to himself, "The bowl is the easy part. It's the waiting that's hard. It's the not-knowing-if. Mrs. Crumb is very brave. Braver than she knows."

And Pip, who was eleven, who had a label maker, who was learning the difference between a thing that is lost and a thing that is gone, filed that too, with a little star on the tag.

He did not know, then, that the biscuit-colored dog was alive, and fat, and happy, and asleep, at that very moment, under the proofing rack at The Warm Loaf, three streets from the man who had stopped coming in. He did not know that the bowl had been empty every morning for eight years, and that Mrs. Crumb refilled it every night, and that the mud by the door was real.

He only knew that the bowl was full, and that somebody drank from it, and that leaving it out was a kind of love that did not need to own the thing it loved.

It was, he thought, the bravest thing he had ever seen. And he had seen a six-year-old put his shoes on the wrong feet on purpose.

## Chapter 10 - The Umbrella Orchard (Please Do Not Water)

By the end of the third day, Pip had a job.

He had not meant to get a job. He had meant to love Captain Chomp back to remembering and then go home. But the Bureau was enormous and the staff were few, and there is a law - older than forms, older than the door - that when a willing boy stands in a room full of work, the work finds him.

It started with the dusting. Barnaby handed him a cloth and said, "The things like to be dusted. It reminds them they are still things. A thing that is not dusted begins to feel like a memory. We do not want that." So Pip dusted. He dusted the single gloves and the jar of humming keys and the spectacle frames with no lenses, and he found that dusting was not cleaning, exactly. It was a way of saying *I see you. You are still here. You are still a thing.* And the things, he could have sworn, stood a little straighter under the cloth.

Then it was the labeling. Miss Whisk, who did not like visitors, took one look at Pip's label maker and said, "Huh. A machine that makes the record smaller." And she let him - actually let him - make tags for the new intake, because her paw cramped in the evenings and she would not admit it. Pip printed tags in his careful block letters, and Miss Whisk corrected his spelling without looking, and they worked in a silence that was, Pip realized, the comfortable silence of two people who both believed that writing a thing down was a way of keeping it.

And then it was the waiting.

This was the part Pip had not understood, the part Barnaby had said was the important part. Every thing in the Bureau needed to be waited with. Not all the time. But some of the time. A thing that was waited with remembered. So Pip sat with them. He sat with the red mitten and told it about the grandmother who had knitted it, which he did not know, so he made up a grandmother who was probably close, and the mitten seemed to like it. He sat with the toy car with one wheel and told it about being small, which he did know, and the car's one wheel turned a little, as if remembering a specific carpet. He sat with the spectacle frame and read to it, the way the man used to read to his dog, and he read the labels on the nearby shelves, which was not a story, but was, he thought, a kind of story, the story of all the things that were waiting.

And in between, he saw the wonders.

He saw the umbrella orchard, which was a whole grove of furled umbrellas standing in rows like trees, and when the light hit them they made a sound like rain that wasn't there, and the sign said PLEASE DO NOT WATER, and Goldie told him that once a pigeon had watered them out of spite and they had all bloomed at once and it had taken a week to get them furled again, and Barnaby had been very diplomatic about it but his whiskers had never forgiven the pigeon.

He saw the field of lost cows. It was actually a field, in a room that should not have fit a field, and the cows were gray and patient and mooed softly, and they were all the cows that had ever been lost, which was more cows than Pip would have guessed, and they were waiting for the farmers who had lost them, and some of the farmers had been gone a long time, and the cows did not know that, and the Bureau did not tell them, because the waiting was the kind part.

He saw the jar of keys, which hummed when you passed it, and he asked Barnaby why, and Barnaby said, "They are remembering their locks. A key without a lock is a question. The hum is the question." And Pip thought that was the saddest and most beautiful thing he had ever heard, and he put his hand on the jar, and the hum went through his palm and up his arm, and he felt, for a second, every locked door in Marrow's End, and every person who had ever stood in front of one, patting their pockets.

He saw the drawer of homework excuses, valid and dubious, and he laughed, and Miss Whisk said, "The dubious ones are more honest. The valid ones are trying too hard," and Pip laughed harder.

And every day, twice a day, he sat with Captain Chomp, and Ollie sat with him, and they told the dinosaur about home, about the middle stair and the refrigerator and the pizza on the floor, and Captain Chomp's loose button caught the light a little more each day, and his twice-sewn tail seemed a little less unsure, and once - once - when Ollie did the roar, the dinosaur's whole body seemed to lean into it, the way a plant leans into the sun.

"He's remembering," Barnaby said, watching. "You can see it. The loving is paving the way."

And Pip, dusting and labeling and waiting, realized something that he had to sit down on a lost step (STEP. STILL LOOKING.) to think about.

He had come to Marrow's End feeling like a lost thing. He had labeled his shoe STILL HERE because he had not been sure it was true. He had labeled the house HOME because he had not been sure it was. He had been a thing set down somewhere and not picked back up, a single sock in a town of dryers, waiting to be claimed.

And now he had a job. He had a title, Friend of the Lost, which was not official but was true. He had a cat who corrected his spelling and a hedgehog who gave him spare buttons and a walrus who made up contracts and a girl who took him through fences. He had a dinosaur to love back and a brother to be brave for and a bowl to remember and a record to keep.

He was not a lost thing anymore. He was a thing that had been found. Not by being claimed - nobody had claimed Pip. But by being useful. By being waited with. By having a place in the enormous, kindly, slightly chaotic room where the lost things were kept, and by being, himself, one of the keepers.

Feeling lost, he understood now, was not the same as being unloved. He had felt lost, and he had been loved the whole time - by his mother with the tired-and-proud look, by his father who wrestled chairs, by Ollie who put his shoes on the wrong feet on purpose. The lost feeling had been a fog. The loving had been the ground under the fog. It had been there the whole time. He just hadn't been able to see it, because he had been new, and new is a kind of lost, and lost is a kind of fog.

He sat on the lost step and he felt the ground under the fog, and it was solid, and it was warm, and it was his.

"Pip," said Ollie, appearing with Captain Chomp under his arm, because it was going-home time. "Ready?"

"Ready," said Pip.

They said goodnight to Barnaby, who was leaking contentedly, and to Miss Whisk, who said, "Don't let the door hit you. It's shy, but it's also heavy," and to Thimble, who was doing the rain ones and did not look up but raised one quill in salute, the buttons clicking. And they went to the little green door, and Pip put his hand on the brass knob, and he paused.

"Can I label it?" he asked. "The door. So I can find it again. So I know it's here."

Barnaby considered this. "The door finds you," he said. "You know that. You don't need a label."

"I know," said Pip. "But I want to give it one. Not so I can find it. So it knows it's found."

Barnaby's small eyes went very soft. "Yes," he said. "I think that's allowed. I think that's very allowed."

So Pip printed a label, and he pressed it onto the little green door, right under the brass plate that said BUREAU OF LOST THINGS. PLEASE KNOCK. And the label said:

FOUND. COMES BACK TO.

He looked at it, and it looked back, and he felt the ground under the fog, solid and warm.

Then they stepped through, into the schoolyard, into the dusk, into Marrow's End, and the door swung shut behind them, and the smell of dust and lavender and old paper went out like a candle.

Pip did not notice, then, that the label was slightly crooked. He did not notice that it would matter, later, that it was crooked, that a crooked label on a door that moves is a thing you can follow. He only noticed that the door was green, and that it was found, and that he would be back tomorrow.

And from somewhere under the hill, very faintly, something hummed. It was a patient hum. A keeping hum. The hum of a machine that had decided, quietly, that nothing it loved would ever be lost again.

Pip heard it, and stopped, and looked down the hill.

"Pip?" said Ollie. "What is it?"

"Nothing," said Pip. "I thought I heard... nothing."

They went home. And the humming went on, patient and keeping, under the hill, under the house with too many curtains, getting ready.

But that was tomorrow's problem. Tonight there was a dinosaur who was almost remembering, and a label that said FOUND, and a boy who had stopped being a lost thing.

Tonight, that was enough.

# Part II

## Chapter 11 - The Shelf That Forgot Its Shelf

On the fourth day, the music box was still gone, and now the thing next to it was gone too.

Pip saw it the moment he came through the green door, because he had learned the shelves the way you learn a face, and a face with a missing tooth is a face you notice. The clean square in the dust where the music box had been was still there. But beside it, where there had been a small brass telescope - *One telescope. Belonged to a boy who wanted to see the moon. Claimed. Ready.* - there was now a second clean square.

Two clean squares. Two tags. No things.

"Miss Whisk," said Pip, and his voice came out thinner than he meant it to.

Miss Whisk was already there. She was standing in front of the shelf with the great ledger open in her paws, and she was not stamping, and she was not being sarcastic, and that, more than the clean squares, made Pip's stomach go cold.

"I know," she said. "I've been counting."

"Counting what?"

"The discrepancies." She turned the ledger so he could see. There were marks in the margin, small precise marks, and there were a great many of them. "A discrepancy is when the record says a thing is here, and the thing is not here. The record is the truth. So when there is a discrepancy, either the truth is wrong, or the thing is wrong." She looked at Pip over her spectacles. "The truth is not wrong. I have checked. I have checked four times. The things are wrong. The things are gone."

Barnaby came up the aisle at his best speed, which for a walrus was a kind of urgent waddle, his waistcoat pockets swinging, his leaking pen forgotten. He looked at the two clean squares, and his whiskers went down, and he made a sound like a kettle deciding not to boil.

"The telescope," he said. "The boy who wanted to see the moon. He was going to get it on Finding Day. He's been waiting. He's been so patient." He put a flipper on the empty shelf, gently, the way you'd touch a shoulder. "He was ready."

"Things that are ready don't leave," said Miss Whisk. "That's the point of ready. Ready means going home. It does not mean going... elsewhere." She said the word *elsewhere* like it was a word she had found on the floor and did not want to hold.

Pip looked down the aisle. The Bureau was quiet, the full-quiet, the library-quiet. But now the quiet had a different shape. It was the quiet of a room where something had been taken, and the something had not left a note, and the room did not know how to say so.

"How many?" Pip asked.

Miss Whisk looked at her marks. "Since the first of the week," she said, "seventeen."

"Seventeen things?"

"Seventeen things that were claimed and ready and going home on Finding Day." She closed the ledger, and the sound was very loud in the quiet. "Seventeen things that somebody loved, that were loved back, that remembered the way, and that are now... not here. The record says they are here. The shelf says they are not. I do not know how to file that. There is no stamp for that."

Barnaby sat down on a lost step, heavily, and the step said STEP. STILL LOOKING. and Barnaby said, "I know, old friend. I know." And for a moment the Chief of the Bureau of Lost Things looked like what he was, which was a very old, very kind, very tired walrus who had spent his whole life keeping things safe and had just learned that safe was not a thing you could promise.

Pip felt something rise in him. It was not fear, exactly. It was the thing he felt when a label was wrong, when a thing was not where it was supposed to be, when the world needed to be put back in order. It was the label-maker feeling. It was the *I will find this and I will write it down and I will make it right* feeling.

"We need to find them," he said.

"Pip," said Barnaby, gently, "the door is the only way in or out. The door is shy. It only opens for people who have lost something they love. Nothing can come in that does not belong. Nothing can take a thing out. That is the rule. That is the whole rule."

"Then how-"

"I don't know," said Barnaby, and it was the first time Pip had heard the walrus say those words, and they sounded strange in his deep, wet, certain voice, like a cello playing a note it had never played. "I don't know how. And I have been Chief here for a very long time, and I have never not known how."

Ollie, who had been holding Captain Chomp and listening, said, in a small voice, "Is Captain Chomp safe?"

Barnaby looked at Ollie, and he made his face be a thing you could stand on. "Captain Chomp is on his shelf," he said. "He is being dusted. He is being waited with. He is safe." He paused, and he added, because he was honest, "I will put him somewhere closer. Somewhere I can see him. Just to be sure."

And Pip, watching Barnaby waddle off to move a dinosaur to a safer shelf, felt the floor tilt again, and he thought: *safe is not a thing you can promise.* And he thought: *then I will make it a thing you can promise.* And he did not know how, and he was eleven, and he had a label maker, and he had decided things before.

He took out the label maker. He printed a tag, and he pressed it onto the empty shelf, right between the two clean squares. And the tag said:

MISSING. NOT GONE. WE WILL FIND YOU.

Miss Whisk looked at the tag. She looked at Pip. And the corner of her mouth did the almost-smile again, the one that was rarer than a blue moon.

"That's not a real stamp," she said.

"It's a real promise," said Pip.

Miss Whisk was quiet for a moment. Then she reached into her desk, and she took out a stamp Pip had never seen, a stamp with no label on the handle, and she pressed it onto the tag, next to his printing. And the stamp said:

COUNTED.

"Everything that is missing gets counted," she said. "That's the rule I'm making up right now. If it's counted, it's not forgotten. If it's not forgotten, it's not gone." She looked at the two clean squares, and her paw went, once, to her empty collar. "Seventeen," she said. "We count them. We find them. And whoever is doing this-" and her voice went flat and precise and dangerous, the voice of the truth's secretary, "-is going to be very sorry they picked a room full of people who know how to wait."

"I can count," Ollie announced. "I'm very good at counting. I can count to a hundred. And then I can count to a hundred again, which is harder, because you have to remember you already did it."

Miss Whisk looked at Ollie. Then she took a fresh page, and she wrote, at the top, in her precise hand, OFFICIAL COUNTER, and under it, OLLIE PARKER, AGE 6, VERY GOOD AT COUNTING. She handed him a pencil that was almost as big as he was.

"You are now the Official Counter," she said. "It is not an official title. I just made it up. But it is true. Your job is to count the missing things, and to tell me if the number changes. Do not lose the pencil."

"I won't," said Ollie, with enormous seriousness. "I'll label it."

And Pip, watching his brother hold the pencil like a sword, felt the floor stop tilting, just a little, because a room where a six-year-old is given a job is a room that has decided to do something, and a room that has decided to do something is not a room that is lost.

From somewhere under the hill, very faintly, came the humming. It was a patient hum. A keeping hum. And this time, Pip did not think it was nothing. This time, Pip listened, and he filed the sound in the drawer where he kept the things that mattered, and he put a little star on the tag.

*Be gentle with this one,* he thought. *But find it. Find it fast.*

The Bureau had seventeen missing things, and one crooked promise, and a cat who had just made up a rule.

It was, Pip thought, enough to start with. It would have to be. Because Finding Day was in four days, and seventeen somebodies were waiting for things that were not there, and the humming under the hill was getting louder.

## Chapter 12 - A Humming in the Key of Missing

The humming was not in Pip's head, and he could prove it, because Ollie could hear it too, and Ollie was six and did not have the kind of head that made things up on purpose.

"It's a bee," said Ollie, the first time.

"It's not a bee," said Pip. "Bees don't hum in the same note for an hour. Bees get bored."

"It's a very patient bee," said Ollie.

But it was not a bee, and by the fourth day Pip had stopped pretending it might be. The humming was always there, at the edge of things, under the floor of the Bureau, under the hill of the town, a low, steady, keeping note, like a refrigerator that had decided to become a religion. It was the sound of something that was never going to stop. And Pip, who had labeled his refrigerator COLD, MOSTLY, knew the sound of a machine that had a job to do, and this machine's job, he was starting to think, was *keep*.

He decided to find it. He decided it the way he decided everything, which was out loud, with a label.

"We are going to find the hum," he told Goldie, in the Bureau, between the umbrella orchard and the jar of keys. "I'm going to label it when I find it. I'm going to write down where it is. And then it won't be a mystery anymore. It'll be a location."

Goldie looked at him with the look of a girl who knew every shortcut and therefore knew that some things did not have shortcuts. "You can't label a sound, Pip."

"I can label where it comes from."

"Sounds come from everywhere. That's the whole thing about sounds."

"Then I'll label everywhere," said Pip, "until the sound runs out of places to be."

Goldie considered this. Then she grinned, the shortcut grin, the *this is a terrible idea and I'm in* grin. "Okay," she said. "Okay, Friend of the Lost. Let's go find your hum. But we do it my way. We don't walk in straight lines. Straight lines are how you get noticed. We go the crooked way."

They went the crooked way.

They started in the Bureau, because the humming was loudest there, under the floor. Pip put his ear to the floor of the sock wing, and the humming came up through the wood, steady, patient, and Thimble, who was doing the rain ones, said, without looking up, "It's been there a week. It's getting louder. The socks don't like it. The socks can hear it better than we can. They've been leaning away from it." And Pip looked, and the socks in the bins nearest the floor were, in fact, leaning, very slightly, all of them, like a field of small gray wheat in a wind that came from below.

"That's not possible," said Pip.

"A lot of things aren't," said Thimble. "The socks lean. I file it under COMPLICATED. Go away. I'm doing the rain ones."

They went outside, into the town, and Goldie took him the crooked way, through the gap in the hedge, past the cat who lived in the gap, down the lane that went uphill. And as they went, Pip listened, and he labeled. He labeled a fence post HUM: LOUDER HERE. He labeled a drain HUM: UNDER. He labeled a particular paving stone, which he was almost sure was the right one, HUM: CLOSE. The labels were a trail, a crooked trail, a trail of small white flags that said *the sound is this way*, and Goldie watched him do it and said, "You know you're making a map of a noise."

"Yes," said Pip.

"That's the most Pip thing I've ever seen," said Goldie, and she said it with such fondness that Pip's ears went warm, and he kept labeling.

The trail went uphill. It went toward the house with too many curtains. Pip noticed this, and his stomach did a small thing, and he kept labeling, because labeling was a thing you could do with your hands when your stomach was doing things.

And then, at the corner of the lane, just below the curtained house, the humming stopped.

It did not fade. It did not get quieter. It stopped, all at once, the way a radio stops when someone unplugs it, and the silence that replaced it was so sudden and so complete that Pip and Goldie both froze, mid-step, like two animals who have just realized they are not alone in the field.

"It knows we're listening," said Goldie, very quietly.

"Machines don't know things," said Pip, but his voice was quiet too.

"This one does," said Goldie. "Listen."

They listened. The silence had a shape. It was the silence of something holding its breath. It was the silence of the house with too many curtains, and it was the same silence, exactly the same, and Pip felt the two silences click together like two halves of a thing that had been broken.

Then, from the house, very faintly, came a sound that was not the humming. It was a click. A small, mechanical click. And then another. And then a whir, soft, like a thing turning over in its sleep, and then the humming came back, but quieter now, careful, the way you hum when you are trying not to be heard.

"It turned itself down," said Goldie. "Pip. It turned itself down so we wouldn't hear it. Machines don't do that."

Pip looked up at the house. Every window was curtained. Every curtain was drawn. The house was holding its breath, and under the breath, under the floor, under the hill, something was keeping, keeping, keeping, and it had just noticed two children on the lane, and it had decided to be quiet.

"We should go," said Goldie, and for the first time since Pip had known her, the shortcut girl did not sound like she was enjoying the shortcut.

They went. They went the crooked way, back down the hill, and Pip did not label anything on the way back, because his hands were busy holding the thing he had learned, which was too big for a label. He held it all the way to the Bureau, and he set it down in front of Barnaby, and he said, "The hum comes from the Snell place. And it knows when we're listening. And it turned itself down."

Barnaby was quiet for a long moment. His whiskers moved. His leaking pen dripped, once, onto the intake log, and he did not dab at it.

"The Snell place," he said, softly. "Silas Snell." He said the name the way you say a name you have not said in a long time, a name that has a story attached, a name that is heavy with the story. "I knew him once. Before. He was a maker. An inventor. He made things that... " Barnaby paused, and his small eyes went far away. "He made things to keep. He was always making things to keep. Even then. Even before he lost the dog." He looked at Pip. "I had hoped he was just a sad man in a curtained house. I had hoped the hum was nothing. I am the Chief of the Bureau of Lost Things, and I had hoped, because hoping is easier than knowing."

"What does he want?" Pip asked.

Barnaby looked at the seventeen marks in Miss Whisk's ledger, at the two clean squares in the dust, at the crooked trail of labels that led uphill.

"I think," he said, slowly, "he wants what everyone wants. He wants the things he loves to stay. He wants them to never be lost. He wants them to never leave." He sighed, the long, soggy, contented sigh, but this time it was not contented. "And I think he has built a way to make that happen. And I think, Pip, that a man who has built a way to keep everything he loves is the most dangerous kind of man there is."

"Dangerous?" said Pip. "But he's just sad. Mrs. Crumb said he's just sad."

"Yes," said Barnaby. "That's what makes him dangerous. Not because he's cruel. Because he's sad, and he's clever, and he has decided that the answer to losing things is to never let them go. And that answer, Pip, is a machine. And machines do not know when to stop."

The humming came up through the floor, patient, keeping, careful. And Pip, who was eleven, who had a label maker, who had just mapped a noise uphill to a house with too many curtains, felt the ground under the fog go a little cold, and he thought: *a machine that does not know when to stop.*

He thought of Goldie's pocket, of the marble and the paperclip and the rubber band, of *things go, that's what things do.* He thought of Mrs. Crumb's bowl, left out for a stray she did not own. He thought of the difference between leaving a bowl out and closing all the curtains.

And he thought: *we have to find out what that machine is. Before Finding Day. Before it keeps something it can't give back.*

"Tomorrow," he said. "Tomorrow we find out what it is."

Barnaby nodded, slowly, and his whiskers moved, and the humming went on, patient and keeping, under the hill, under the house, turning itself down when it was listened to, turning itself up when it was not.

It was, Pip thought, the most patient thing he had ever met. And he had met a walrus who could wait for a cow.

## Chapter 13 - The Trail of Nervous Buttons

Ollie, who was the Official Counter, found the first button, because Official Counters look at the floor, and the floor is where the clues are.

He was counting the missing things - he was up to seventeen, and he had counted to seventeen four times to be sure, and he had labeled his pencil OFFICIAL, DO NOT LOSE - when he saw it, by the leg of Miss Whisk's desk. A button. A small brown button, four-holed, lying on the floor like it had fallen off something and was too embarrassed to mention it.

"Button," said Ollie, and he picked it up, and he held it up, and he said, "This is not a missing thing. This is a found thing. Do I count found things? The rule says missing things. I need a ruling."

Miss Whisk, who was deep in the ledger, said, without looking, "Count everything. The rule is whatever I say it is. Bring it here."

Ollie brought it. Miss Whisk took the button, and she looked at it, and her spectacles caught the lamplight, and she went very still.

"This is a coat button," she said. "A nervous one."

"A nervous button?" said Pip.

"Buttons come in two kinds," said Miss Whisk, and she set the button on the desk with the care of someone setting down evidence. "There are the buttons that stay. And there are the buttons that pop. A button that pops is a button that was on a thing that was pulled. A thing that is pulled - pulled hard, pulled sudden - its buttons pop. They fly. They land. They roll." She looked at the button. "This button was on a coat. The coat was pulled. The button popped. And the coat went... elsewhere. And the button stayed."

"Seventeen things," said Pip. "Seventeen things went elsewhere. And they left their buttons."

"Not all of them," said Miss Whisk. "But some. The ones with coats. The ones with buttons." She was already moving, already scanning the floor, her precise eyes missing nothing. "If a thing was pulled, and its buttons popped, then the buttons would fall along the way. Along the path. The path the thing was taken." She looked up. "There would be a trail."

There was a trail.

They found it together, the four of them - Pip and Ollie and Goldie and Miss Whisk, and Barnaby waddling behind, and Thimble summoned from the sock wing, grumbling about the rain ones. They found the buttons one by one, then in twos and threes, a crooked line of them leading from the back of the Bureau, from the great door that none of them had ever seen open, out into the alley, down the lane, uphill.

A blue button. A brass button. A little pearl button. A red one shaped like a star. Each one a piece of a coat that had been pulled. Each one a clue that said *this way, the thing went this way.*

Thimble picked up the red star button and held it to the light, and his quills clicked, and he made a small sound.

"I know this button," he said.

"You do?" said Pip.

"I gave this button," said Thimble. "I gave it to a coat. A small coat. A child's coat, came in lost, missing a button, and I gave it this one, from my quills, and I sewed it on, and I said the promise. *I expect you to come loose, and I expect to fix you, and I am not going anywhere.*" He held the red star button in his palm, and his small black eyes were bright. "And now the button is here. On the floor. Popped. Which means the coat was pulled. Which means the coat went elsewhere. Which means-" and his voice did a thing, a small, prickly, furious thing, "-which means somebody undid my promise."

The silence that followed had a temperature. Thimble, who was small and round and the color of toast, who sorted socks by smell and gave spare buttons to lost coats, stood there holding the red star button, and he was, Pip realized, angrier than anyone in the Bureau, because the thing that had been taken was not just a coat. It was a promise. It was a *I am not going anywhere.* And somebody had gone somewhere with it.

"We follow the trail," said Thimble. "Now. We follow it to the end. And we find whoever undid my promise. And we have a word."

"Thimble," said Barnaby, gently, "we will follow the trail. But we will be careful. We will be kind. We do not know yet what we are walking toward."

"I know what I'm walking toward," said Thimble. "I'm walking toward my button. The rest of you can walk toward whatever you like." And he set off down the trail, his quills clicking, the red star button clutched in his paw, and the rest of them followed, because you did not argue with Thimble, and because, Pip thought, sometimes the smallest person in the room is the one who has decided.

They followed the buttons uphill. Goldie took the crooked way, but the buttons took the straight way, and for once Goldie followed the buttons instead of the shortcuts, because the buttons were a map that did not need a shortcut. The trail went past the fence post Pip had labeled HUM: LOUDER HERE. It went past the drain HUM: UNDER. It went past the paving stone HUM: CLOSE. And Pip, following, felt the two maps click together, the map of the noise and the map of the buttons, both of them pointing the same way, both of them saying *uphill, uphill, to the house with too many curtains.*

The trail ended at the gate of the Snell place.

The last button - a big brass one, the kind that holds a heavy coat together - lay at the foot of the gate, and beyond the gate the path went up to the house, and every window was curtained, and every curtain was drawn, and the house was holding its breath.

And from under the house, under the hill, came the humming. Patient. Keeping. Careful. It had turned itself down, the way it did when it was listened to, but it was there, and it was loud enough now that Pip could feel it in his teeth, and he understood, with a certainty that went from his stomach to his shoes, that the humming was the machine, and the machine was the thing that had pulled the coats, and the coats had gone into the house, and the buttons had popped and fallen and rolled downhill, all the way to the Bureau, all the way to Miss Whisk's desk, all the way to Ollie's Official Counter eyes.

"Seventeen things," said Pip. "They're in there. They're all in there."

"Seventeen things," said Miss Whisk, "and one red star button, and one promise." She looked at the gate. "And one man who thinks he's saving them."

Pip looked at her. "Saving them?"

Miss Whisk was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "A man who takes a thing and keeps it safe and never lets it go - he doesn't call that stealing. He calls that saving. He calls that love." Her paw went to her empty collar. "I know the difference. I keep the file on the difference. It's in the back. It's administrative." She looked at the curtained house. "But I'll tell you this, Friend of the Lost. The difference between saving a thing and keeping a thing is whether the thing gets to go home. And nothing in that house is going home. I can smell it. And I don't even sort the socks."

Thimble, who did sort the socks, said, quietly, "She's right. The buttons smell like kept. Not like lost. Lost smells like waiting. Kept smells like... never." He shivered, and his quills clicked. "I don't like never. Never is not a smell a thing should have."

They stood at the gate, the five of them and Ollie, and they looked up at the house with too many curtains, and the humming went on, patient and keeping, and Pip felt the ground under the fog go cold and solid at the same time, because now he knew where, and now he knew what, and the only thing left was the who and the why, and the who was a sad man named Silas Snell, and the why was a dog that ran off eight years ago.

"Tomorrow," said Pip. "Tomorrow we knock."

"Tomorrow," Barnaby agreed. "But tonight, we count. Tonight, we make sure Captain Chomp is safe. And tonight, we remember that the man in that house is not a monster. He is a man who lost something he loved, and could not bear it, and built a machine so he would never have to bear it again." He looked at Pip with his small, kind eyes. "That is not a monster, Pip. That is a heartbreak with a workshop. And heartbreaks are harder to fight than monsters, because you cannot punch a heartbreak. You can only... " He paused, and he searched for the word, and he found it. "You can only answer it."

"Answer it with what?" said Pip.

Barnaby was quiet for a long moment. Then he said, "I don't know yet. But I think you will. I think you are the one who will." He turned, and his flippers made the sound of two wet hands applauding, and he waddled back down the hill, and the rest of them followed.

And behind them, in the house with too many curtains, the humming turned itself up, just a little, the way a machine does when it thinks no one is listening anymore.

It had seventeen things. It had one red star button's worth of promise. And it was getting ready for Finding Day.

But that was tomorrow's problem. Tonight there was a trail of nervous buttons, and a small hedgehog holding a red star, and a boy who had decided to knock.

Tonight, that was enough.

## Chapter 14 - The Case Board (Now with Labels)

Pip built the case board on the back of a lost door.

The door was lying flat in the Bureau, labeled DOOR. FORMERLY A DOOR. NOW A TABLE., and it was the perfect size for a case board, which Pip knew because he had seen case boards in books, and case boards were where detectives put the things they knew so the things they knew could look at each other and become the thing they didn't know yet.

He used his label maker. He used it the way a painter uses a brush, the way Miss Whisk uses her stamps, with a kind of furious grace. He printed the things they knew, and he stuck them to the door, and he connected them with pieces of string that Goldie found in her pocket, because Goldie's pocket had string in it, of course it did.

THE HUM," said one label. "COMES FROM THE SNELL PLACE. KNOWS WHEN WE LISTEN. TURNS ITSELF DOWN."

THE BUTTONS," said another. "NERVOUS ONES. POPPED OFF COATS THAT WERE PULLED. TRAIL LEADS UPHILL."

SEVENTEEN THINGS," said a third. "CLAIMED. READY. GOING HOME ON FINDING DAY. NOW ELSEWHERE."

And in the middle, in big letters, the thing they didn't know yet:

WHY?

"WHY is the important one," said Pip, stepping back. "WHO is Silas Snell. WHERE is the house. WHAT is a machine. But WHY is the one we need. Because if we know WHY, we know how to answer it. Barnaby said you can't punch a heartbreak. You can only answer it. So we need to know what the heartbreak is asking."

Goldie looked at the case board. "That's a lot of string for one why."

"Why is a big question."

"It's the biggest," Goldie agreed. "Okay. What do we know about the why?"

They sat around the lost door, the five of them and Ollie, and they pooled what they knew, and Miss Whisk wrote it down in the ledger because the record was the truth, and Barnaby leaked on it, and nobody minded.

What they knew was this: Silas Snell was an inventor. He made things to keep. Eight years ago his dog Biscuit ran away. He looked for a while. He put up posters. The posters got rained on. He took them down. He stopped coming in to the bakery. He closed all the curtains. And somewhere in those eight years, he built a machine that hummed, and the machine took lost things, and it kept them, and it did not let them go home.

"So the why is the dog," said Ollie, who had been listening with his whole face. "He's sad about the dog. So he's keeping everything. So nothing else runs away."

The silence that followed was the kind where everyone looks at the six-year-old, because the six-year-old has just said the whole thing, simply, the way six-year-olds do, without the string and the labels and the case board.

"Yes," said Barnaby, softly. "Yes, Ollie. That's the why. He lost the dog, and he could not bear it, and he decided that the answer was to make a world where nothing is ever lost. Where everything stays. Where nothing runs away." He sighed. "It is a very old answer. It is the answer everyone wants, for about a minute. And then they learn that it is not an answer. It is a cage. But he has not learned that yet. He has been building the cage for eight years, and he thinks it is a home."

"A cage," said Goldie, and her hand went to her pocket, to the marble and the paperclip and the rubber band, and she patted them, the way you pat a thing you are afraid of losing. "He's caging them."

"He thinks he's saving them," said Miss Whisk. "That's the trouble. He thinks the cage is a home. And the things in it - they're not waiting anymore. They're kept. And kept is not waiting. Kept is... " She searched for the word. "Kept is waiting that has been told to stop."

Pip looked at the case board, at the WHY in the middle, and he felt the shape of the problem settle into him, the way a label settles onto a thing. The problem was not a monster. The problem was a man who loved so much he could not let go, and a machine that loved the same way, and seventeen things - and Captain Chomp - caught in the loving.

"So we have to teach him," said Pip. "That it's okay to let go. That things come back. That... " He thought of Goldie's pocket. "That the sad is the good kind, if you let it be."

"Yes," said Barnaby. "But you cannot teach a man who has built a machine to avoid the lesson. The machine is the lesson he refused to learn. And the machine is getting stronger. And Finding Day is in four days." He looked at the case board. "So we need to understand the machine. Not just the why. The what. The how. Because if we are going to answer the heartbreak, we need to know what the heartbreak has built."

It was then that Ollie, who was the Official Counter, and who therefore looked at the floor, said, "There's a thing under the door."

They looked. Under the lost door, where it lay flat on the floor, there was a small brass object, half in the dust. Pip reached under and pulled it out.

It was a gear. A small brass gear, the size of a coin, with teeth around its edge and a hole in the middle. And on its face, engraved in tiny, careful letters, was a word:

KEEPER.

"The Keeper," said Pip. He held the gear up, and the lamplight caught it, and it turned, slowly, in his fingers, though there was no reason for it to turn. "That's what the machine is called. The Keeper."

"How do you know it's the machine's name and not just a word?" Goldie asked.

"Because it's engraved on a gear that fell off a machine that keeps things," said Pip. "And because..." He turned the gear over. On the back, in the same tiny letters, was a second line: *SO NOTHING I LOVE IS EVER LOST AGAIN.*

The silence that followed had a temperature, and the temperature was sad.

"He wrote it on the gear," said Miss Whisk, quietly. "He wrote the why on the gear. So the machine would remember. So even the machine would know what it was for." Her paw went to her empty collar. "That's not a monster's machine. That's a... " She stopped. She did not finish. But Pip heard the finish anyway. *That's a love letter. He built a love letter to a dog, and the love letter is eating the town.*

Barnaby took the gear gently in his flipper, and he looked at it for a long time, and his whiskers moved.

"The Keeper," he said. "So nothing I love is ever lost again." He set the gear on the case board, right under the WHY, and he connected it with a piece of Goldie's string. "Now we know its name. And its why. And its how, a little. A machine that keeps. A machine that pulls. A machine that does not know when to stop." He looked at Pip. "You have built a good case board, Friend of the Lost. But a case board is not an answer. It is a question, arranged neatly. And we are running out of days to answer it."

Pip looked at the case board, at the labels and the string and the gear and the WHY, and he felt the ground under the fog, solid and cold. He had a name. He had a why. He had a gear. He had four days.

"Tomorrow," he said. "Tomorrow we find out what the Keeper does. Exactly. We watch it. We see it take something. And then we'll know the how."

"Tomorrow," Barnaby agreed. "But tonight, we move Captain Chomp. Tonight, we put him somewhere the Keeper cannot reach. Because the Keeper takes the things that are loved, and Captain Chomp is loved, and I will not have him kept."

And Pip, who was eleven, who had a label maker, who had just built a case board on a lost door, felt the thing he had been feeling all week settle into a decision, hard and clear, like a label pressed onto a thing that was not going anywhere.

He was going to answer the heartbreak. He was going to teach the machine to stop. He was going to get seventeen things home for Finding Day, and one dinosaur, and one red star button's worth of promise.

He did not know how. He was eleven. But he had a case board, and a gear, and a why, and four days, and a brother who was very good at counting.

It was, he thought, enough to start with. It would have to be. Because under the hill, the Keeper hummed, patient and keeping, and it had just noticed that one of its gears was missing.

And a machine that keeps things does not like to lose its own.

## Chapter 15 - The Things That Were Loved Best

On the fifth day, the number changed, and Ollie, who was the Official Counter, was the one who noticed, because Official Counters notice when the number changes.

"Eighteen," he said. He was standing in front of the shelf with his pencil and his page, and his face was doing the thing it did when a number was wrong, which was the same thing it did when a dinosaur was lost. "It was seventeen. I counted it four times. It was seventeen. And now it's eighteen. And I didn't even count a new one. It just... became eighteen."

Pip came to the shelf. There was a new clean square in the dust. A new tag. *One harmonica. Plays a song that sounds like a train. Belonged to a man who played it to his plants. Claimed. Ready. Going home on Finding Day.* And no harmonica.

"It took another one," said Pip.

"It took another one," Miss Whisk agreed. She was already marking the ledger, already making the small precise mark in the margin, and her paw was steady but her ears were flat. "In the night. While we were all here. While the door was shut. While the rule was the rule." She looked at the great door at the back of the Bureau, the one that none of them had ever seen open. "The door is the only way in or out. The door was shut. And yet."

"And yet," Barnaby agreed, heavily. He had moved Captain Chomp in the night, to a shelf right behind his own desk, where he could see him, where his flipper could rest on the dinosaur while he worked. Captain Chomp sat there now, being dusted, being waited with, his loose button catching the light. He was safe. He was as safe as a thing could be in a room where safe was not a thing you could promise.

Pip looked at the eighteen clean squares, and he looked at the shelves around them, and he felt the label-maker feeling, the *there is a pattern here and I will find it* feeling. He walked the aisle. He looked at the things that were gone, and he looked at the things that were still there, and he compared them, the way you compare two lists to find the thing that is on one and not the other.

And then he saw it.

"Miss Whisk," he said. "The things that are gone. They're all... claimed."

Miss Whisk looked up. "What?"

"The things that are gone. The music box. The telescope. The harmonica. All the eighteen. They're all claimed. They're all ready. They're all going home on Finding Day." He pointed down the aisle. "But the things that are still here - the ones that aren't claimed, the ones that are just... waiting, the ones nobody's come for - they're all still here. Every one. The Keeper didn't take a single unclaimed thing."

The silence that followed was the kind where a pattern clicks into place, and the click is loud.

Miss Whisk turned the ledger. She checked. She checked the eighteen, and she checked the unclaimed, and she checked four times, because she was Miss Whisk and the truth was not wrong. And then she set the ledger down, and she said, in a voice that was very flat and very precise, "He's right. The Keeper takes the claimed. The ready. The ones that are loved back. The ones that remember the way home." She looked at Pip over her spectacles. "It does not take the unloved. It does not take the forgotten. It takes the things that are loved best."

"Why?" said Goldie. "Why only the loved ones?"

Barnaby was quiet for a long moment. His whiskers moved. His flipper rested on Captain Chomp, and the dinosaur's loose button caught the light.

"Because," he said, slowly, "the Keeper is a machine that keeps. And a machine that keeps does not want the things nobody wants. It wants the things that are wanted. It wants the love. It wants the... " He searched for the word. "The warmth. A thing that is loved is warm. A thing that is claimed is warm. And the Keeper is cold, and it is trying to be warm, and it is taking the warm things and putting them inside itself, and it thinks that will make it warm." He looked at the eighteen clean squares. "It is not stealing things. It is stealing the loving. It is taking the things that are loved best, because it wants to be loved best, and it does not know that you cannot get loved by keeping. You can only get loved by letting go."

The silence that followed had a temperature, and the temperature was the temperature of a machine that was cold and trying to be warm, and of a man who was lonely and trying to be not-lonely, and of eighteen things that were warm and were now kept.

Pip felt the ground under the fog go cold, because he understood, now, the thing that made the Keeper dangerous, and it was not that it was cruel. It was that it was hungry. It was hungry for the exact thing that Captain Chomp was full of. Captain Chomp, who was loved back, who was claimed, who was remembering the way home, who was warm. Captain Chomp was the warmest thing in the Bureau. And the Keeper took the warm things.

"Barnaby," said Pip. "Captain Chomp. He's... he's the warmest. He's claimed. He's ready. He's almost remembering. He's exactly what the Keeper takes."

Barnaby's flipper tightened on the dinosaur, gently, the way you hold a thing you are afraid of losing. "I know," he said. "That is why he is behind my desk. That is why I do not leave him. That is why I am the Chief, and why I have been the Chief for a very long time, and why I will be the Chief until this is answered." He looked at Pip with his small, kind eyes. "The Keeper will come for him. It will come for the warmest thing. And when it does, I will be here. And I will say no. And we will see whether a machine that keeps things can keep a thing that a walrus is holding."

It was a brave thing to say, and it was a sad thing to say, and Pip loved Barnaby for saying it, and he was also, for the first time, a little afraid for Barnaby, because a walrus is very large and very kind, but a machine that does not know when to stop is a machine that does not know when to stop, and kindness is not a thing you can hold against a gear.

"We have to answer it first," said Pip. "Before it comes for Chomp. Before Finding Day. We have to teach it to stop."

"Yes," said Barnaby. "But we do not yet know how. And the machine is getting stronger. It took a thing in the night, while the door was shut. It is reaching further. It is... " He paused, and he looked at the case board, at the gear under the WHY. "It is missing a gear. And a machine that is missing a gear reaches harder. It compensates. It pulls. It is pulling harder than it was."

Pip felt the thing he had been afraid of settle into him. The gear. The gear they had taken. The Keeper had noticed its gear was missing, and it was reaching harder, and it was taking more, and it was going to take the warmest thing, and the warmest thing was a small green dinosaur who roared in his sleep.

"Three days," said Pip. "Finding Day is in three days. We have three days to answer a heartbreak and teach a machine to stop."

"Three days," Barnaby agreed. "And a case board. And a gear. And a why. And a six-year-old who is very good at counting."

Ollie, who was the Official Counter, looked up from his page. "Eighteen," he said. "I'm going to keep counting. And when it goes back down - when the things come home - I'm going to count them down. And I'm going to count all the way to zero. And zero is the best number. Zero means nothing's missing." He looked at Pip with his whole face. "I'm going to count to zero, Pip. I promise. That's my job. I'm the Official Counter. I count to zero."

And Pip, who was eleven, who had a label maker, who had a brother who was very good at counting, felt the ground under the fog go solid, because a room where a six-year-old promises to count to zero is a room that has decided what winning looks like.

Zero. Nothing missing. Everything home.

"Okay," said Pip. "Okay, Ollie. You count to zero. And I'll find the how." He looked at the case board, at the WHY, at the gear, at the string. "Tomorrow we watch the Keeper. Tomorrow we see it take something. And tomorrow we find the crack in it. Because every machine has a crack. Every cage has a door. And every heartbreak..." He thought of Goldie's pocket, of the marble and the paperclip and the rubber band, of *the sad is the good kind, if you let it be.* "Every heartbreak has an answer. We just have to find it."

Under the hill, the Keeper hummed, patient and keeping, reaching harder, pulling warmer, and it had eighteen things, and it wanted the nineteenth, and the nineteenth was a small green dinosaur behind a walrus's desk.

But that was tomorrow's problem. Tonight there was a number that was going to go back down, and an Official Counter who was going to count it, and a boy who had decided to find the crack.

Tonight, that was enough.

## Chapter 16 - The File in the Back (Administrative)

Pip found the file by accident, which is how you find the things that matter, because the things that matter are always in the back, under *administrative*, where nobody thinks to look.

He was in the back of the Bureau, looking for a spare dusting cloth, because Captain Chomp needed extra dusting now, being the warmest thing, being the nineteenth the Keeper wanted. The back of the Bureau was where the files were, rows and rows of them, and most of them were labeled in Miss Whisk's precise hand, and one of them was not.

One of them was labeled in a handwriting that was younger and rounder and less precise, a handwriting that had not yet learned to be furious and graceful, a handwriting that was, Pip realized, Miss Whisk's, from before she was Miss Whisk. And the label said:

BELL. ADMINISTRATIVE.

Pip knew he should not open it. He was eleven. He knew the rule about other people's files. But his hand was already on the drawer, because the drawer had a temperature, the temperature of a thing that has been waiting a long time, and Pip, who was learning to hear the floors under lightness, could not leave it alone.

He opened it.

Inside was a single page. And on the page was a drawing, done in pencil, of a bell. A small bell, the kind that goes on a collar. And under the drawing, in the young, round handwriting, was a description:

*One bell. From a collar. The collar was red. The person was a girl. She called me Whisper, because I was quiet, and she said the bell was so she could hear me even when I was quiet. The bell fell off in the tall grass. I looked. I looked for a long time. I did not find it. I found the Bureau instead. The Bureau did not have the bell. The Bureau had me. I stayed. The bell is administrative. I do not wait for it. I merely keep the file.*

Pip read it three times, and the third time he understood.

"I do not wait for it," said a voice behind him, "is a lie. I would like that on the record."

Pip turned. Miss Whisk was standing in the aisle, and her spectacles caught the lamplight, and her paw was on her empty collar, and her face was the face of someone who has been caught holding the thing she pretends not to hold.

"I'm sorry," said Pip. "I was looking for a dusting cloth. I shouldn't have-"

"You should have," said Miss Whisk. "That's why I left it where you'd find it." She walked up the aisle, and she sat down on a lost step, and she patted the step beside her, and Pip sat, because you did not argue with Miss Whisk, and because, he thought, sometimes the cat who keeps the record of everyone's loving needs someone to read her own.

"I was a lost thing," she said. "Did you know that? Nobody tells you. They think the staff were always the staff. But I was a lost thing once. A kitten. Quiet. The girl called me Whisper. She put a bell on me so she could hear me even when I was quiet, because I was very quiet, and she was afraid of losing me in the tall grass." Her paw went to her collar. "And then I was lost. And the bell fell off. And I looked for the bell, and I found the Bureau instead. And Barnaby waited with me. And nobody came. And I stayed. And I became the Head Cataloguer. And I told myself the bell was administrative."

"Is it?" said Pip. "Administrative?"

Miss Whisk was quiet for a long moment. Then she said, "No. It's the opposite of administrative. It's the whole thing. It's the whole reason I keep the record. Because I know what it is to be a thing that is lost, and to have the loving written down, and to wait. And I know what it is to have the waiting go on so long that you start to file it under *administrative*, because *administrative* is a word you can hold, and *I miss her* is not." She looked at Pip over her spectacles. "I keep the file. I tell myself I don't wait. But I wait. I wait a little. I wait the way you keep a bowl out. Not because you think they'll come. Because leaving the bowl out is the only thing left that looks like love."

Pip felt the ground under the fog go warm and sad at the same time, because he understood, now, the thing he had been hearing under Miss Whisk's lightness all week. The floor. The floor was a bell. The floor was a girl who called her Whisper. The floor was a red collar and a tall grass and a waiting that had gone on so long it had learned to call itself administrative.

"Everyone has a bell," he said.

Miss Whisk looked at him. "What?"

"Everyone has a bell," said Pip. "A thing in the back. Under administrative. You have the bell. Mr. Snell has the dog. Mrs. Crumb has the bowl. Thimble has... " He thought. "Thimble has the buttons. He keeps spares because he knows things come loose. That's his bell. That's his way of keeping the bowl out." He looked at Miss Whisk. "And I have... " He thought of his label maker, of STILL HERE, of FOUND, COMES BACK TO. "I have the labels. I label things so they don't get lost. That's my bell. That's my bowl."

Miss Whisk stared at him for a long moment. And then she did the thing rarer than a blue moon: she smiled, actually smiled, not the almost-smile but the real one. For a second she was not the Head Cataloguer. She was Whisper, a quiet kitten loved by a girl in the tall grass.

"Yes," she said. "Everyone has a bell. That's the whole thing, Pip. That's the whole thing about the Bureau, and about the Keeper, and about the man in the curtained house. Everyone has a bell. And the question is what you do with the bell. You can keep the file and call it administrative and wait a little. Or you can build a machine so you never have to lose another bell. Or you can..." She paused. "Or you can leave the bowl out. And let the bowl be enough."

"Mr. Snell built a machine," said Pip. "Because he couldn't leave the bowl out. Because the bowl wasn't enough. Because losing the dog was too much, and he decided he would never lose anything again."

"Yes," said Miss Whisk. "And that is the most understandable thing in the world. And it is also the thing that is eating the town. Because you cannot answer a bell by building a cage. You can only answer a bell by... " She searched for the word. "By letting the bell be a bell. By letting the lost thing be lost, and the love stay. The love stays, Pip. Even when the bell is gone. The love stays. That's the thing he doesn't know. That's the thing the machine doesn't know. The love stays."

Pip sat on the lost step, and he held the thing she had given him, which was too big for a label, and he turned it over, and he felt the shape of the answer start to form.

The love stays. Even when the bell is gone. Even when the dog runs off. Even when the thing is lost. The love stays. And the Keeper was taking the things because it thought the love was in the things, and it was not. The love was in the people. And you could not keep the love by keeping the things. You could only keep the love by letting the things go, and letting the love stay.

"Miss Whisk," he said. "I think I know how to answer the heartbreak."

Miss Whisk looked at him. "Do you?"

"I think so. I think... " He thought of Goldie's pocket, of the marble and the paperclip and the rubber band, of *the sad is the good kind, if you let it be.* "I think we have to show him that the love stays. That he can let the things go and still have the love. That Biscuit... " He paused, because he did not know about Biscuit, not really, not the whole thing. "That the dog is not the love. The dog is the thing the love wears. And the love is still there. Even after eight years. Even with the curtains closed."

Miss Whisk was quiet for a long moment. Then she reached out, and she put her paw on Pip's hand, which surprised them both.

"Then you are the one who will answer it," she said. "I knew it when you asked to see the record. A boy who understands that the record keeps the loving, not the things - that boy can answer a heartbreak." She stood, and she smoothed her fur, and she was the Head Cataloguer again, but the almost-smile was still there, in the corner, permanent now. "Go love your dinosaur. And Pip?"

"Yes?"

"Put the file back. Under administrative." She paused, and her paw went to her empty collar, and she said, very quietly, "But leave the drawer open a little. So it gets some air. So it knows it's not forgotten. That's... that's the bowl. That's my bowl. Leave it open a little."

And Pip put the file back, and he left the drawer open a little, and he went to love his dinosaur. He had the answer now, the shape of it. All he needed was the how, and three days.

And under the hill, the Keeper hummed, patient and keeping, not knowing that a boy in the Bureau had just learned the thing it was built to avoid: that the love stays, even when the bell is gone.

## Chapter 17 - Goldie Minds (Just This Once)

The Keeper came for Goldie's pocket on the fifth day, at eleven in the morning, which was rude, because eleven was bun o'clock, and Barnaby was low, and nobody should have to deal with a machine that keeps things on an empty stomach.

It started with the hum. The hum, which was always under the floor, under the hill, came up through the Bureau like a tide, and it was louder than it had ever been, and it was not careful this time, it was not turned down, it was reaching, and the socks in the sock wing leaned away from it, all of them, a whole field of small gray wheat in a wind that came from below.

"It's reaching," said Thimble, looking up from the rain ones. "It's reaching further. The missing gear. It's compensating. I told you. A machine that's missing a gear pulls harder."

And then Goldie's pocket jingled.

It jingled the way it always jingled, the small, satisfied jingle of the marble and the paperclip and the rubber band. But this time the jingle was wrong. This time the jingle was a jingle that was moving, and Goldie looked down, and her face did a thing Pip had never seen it do, which was afraid.

The pocket was pulling. The bulge of it was sliding, toward the seam, toward the hole she'd never fixed, and the marble was rolling, and the paperclip was sliding, and the rubber band was stretching, and all three of them were going, going, toward the hole, toward the floor, toward the hum.

"No," said Goldie.

She grabbed the pocket. She grabbed it with both hands, and she held it shut, and the pull went on, and it was strong, stronger than a pocket should have to fight, and Goldie's heels skidded on the floor, and she said, "No, no, no, you don't, you do not, those are mine-"

"Goldie!" Pip grabbed her arm. Ollie grabbed her other arm, which was not helpful, because Ollie was six and weighed about as much as a determined cat, but he grabbed it anyway, because he was brave in small, loud ways. Barnaby heaved himself up from the desk and put his great gray bulk between Goldie and the floor, between Goldie and the hum, and he said, in his deep, wet, certain voice, "No. You do not. Not in my Bureau. Not while I am the Chief."

And Miss Whisk did the thing that saved them, which was that she stamped the floor.

She stamped it with the DO NOT stamp, the one she used for things that must not, and she stamped it right on the floor where the pull was strongest, and she said, "This is a Bureau. This is a waiting room. This is not a keeping room. And you-" she stamped again, DO NOT, DO NOT, "-are not allowed to keep here. The record says so. And I am the truth's secretary. And the truth says NO."

And the pull stopped.

It did not fade. It stopped, all at once, the way a radio stops when someone unplugs it, and the silence that replaced it was the silence of a machine that has been told no by a cat with a stamp, and the marble rolled back into the pocket, and the paperclip slid back, and the rubber band snapped back into shape, and Goldie's pocket jingled the small, satisfied jingle, and Goldie sat down on the floor, hard, and held her pocket with both hands, and breathed.

The silence went on. And then, from under the hill, very faintly, came a sound that was almost a whine. A small, mechanical whine. The sound of a machine that had wanted something and had been told no, and was thinking about it.

"It's thinking," said Goldie, breathing hard. "Pip. It's thinking about it. It's not going to stop. It's just... thinking about how to try again."

"It won't try again here," said Miss Whisk, and she stamped the floor one more time, DO NOT, for good measure. "The record is the truth. And the truth is that this is a waiting room, and waiting rooms do not keep. It can reach. It can pull. But it cannot take, not while the record says no. Not while I am the secretary." She looked at her stamp, and her paw was shaking, just a little, and she put it behind her back so nobody would see. "But it reached. It reached into the Bureau. It has never reached into the Bureau before. The missing gear. It is getting stronger."

Goldie sat on the floor, and she held her pocket, and she did not let go, and Pip sat down next to her, and Ollie sat down on her other side, and they sat there, the three of them, in the silence that had a temperature.

"I minds," said Goldie, at last. Her voice was small. "I know I said I don't mind. I know I said things go, that's what things do. And I meant it. I mean it. When the acorn went, I didn't mind. When the good acorn went, I shrugged." She held the pocket tighter. "But this. This I mind. This I mind a lot."

"Because it wasn't going," said Pip. "It was being taken."

Goldie looked at him. "Yes," she said. "Yes. That's it. That's the whole thing. When a thing goes, that's... that's the thing being a thing. Things go. But when a thing is taken, when a thing is kept, when a thing is put in a cage and told it can't go home - that's not the thing being a thing. That's somebody being afraid. And I don't want my things to be somebody's afraid." She looked at the pocket, at the marble and the paperclip and the rubber band. "These are mine. They're useless. And they're mine. And I love them. And I don't mind when they go. But I mind when they're kept. And I didn't know there was a difference until just now. And there's a difference. There's a whole difference."

Pip felt the ground under the fog go solid, because Goldie had just said the thing he had been trying to say, the thing that was the answer to the heartbreak, the thing that was the crack in the Keeper. The difference between going and being kept. The difference between letting go and being taken. The difference between a bowl left out and a cage locked shut.

"That's it," he said. "Goldie. That's the answer. That's the thing we have to show him. The difference between going and being kept. He thinks he's saving them. But he's not saving them. He's keeping them. And keeping is not saving. Keeping is... keeping is being afraid, with a machine."

Goldie looked at him. And then she grinned, the shortcut grin, the *this is a terrible idea and I'm in* grin, but this time it had something new in it, something hard and clear, something that had been decided.

"Then we show him," she said. "We show him the difference. And we do it with these." She patted the pocket, and the marble and the paperclip and the rubber band clicked together, the small, satisfied jingle. "These three useless things. They're going to help. I can feel it. They've been waiting for a job. That's why I kept them. That's why they're useless. They're useless so they're ready. They're ready for the one job that matters." She looked at Pip. "Don't ask me how I know. I just know. The pocket knows. The pocket's been knowing things for years. I just listen."

And Pip, who was eleven, who had a label maker, who had a friend whose pocket knew things, felt the shape of the answer settle into him, hard and clear, like a label pressed onto a thing that was not going anywhere.

The marble. The paperclip. The rubber band. Three useless things. Three ready things. He did not know how. He did not know when. But he knew, the way Goldie knew, the way the pocket knew, that they were going to matter, that they were going to be the crack in the Keeper, that they were going to help answer the heartbreak.

"Okay," he said. "Okay. We keep them ready. And we keep Captain Chomp safe. And we find the how." He looked at the floor, where Miss Whisk had stamped DO NOT, and at the great door at the back, and at the hill beyond. "The Keeper reached today. It reached for Goldie's pocket. And it got told no. And it's thinking about how to try again." He looked at Barnaby, who was holding Captain Chomp, who was the warmest thing, who was the nineteenth. "It's going to try again. And next time it's going to reach for the warmest thing. So we have to be ready. We have to answer it first."

"Three days," said Barnaby. "Finding Day is in three days."

"Three days," Pip agreed. "And a pocket full of ready things. And a cat with a stamp. And a walrus who says no. And a six-year-old who counts to zero." He looked at Ollie, who was holding his pencil like a sword. "We've got a lot. We've got enough. We just have to find the how."

Under the hill, the Keeper hummed, patient and keeping, thinking about how to try again, and it had eighteen things, and it wanted the nineteenth, and it had just learned that a cat with a stamp could tell it no.

But a machine that keeps things does not like to be told no. And it was going to remember. And it was going to try again.

But that was tomorrow's problem. Tonight there was a pocket full of ready things, and a girl who had learned the difference between going and being kept, and a boy who had decided to answer a heartbreak.

Tonight, that was enough.

## Chapter 18 - The Night Shift (Everyone Is Bad at Hiding)

They staked out the Bureau that night, and they were all bad at hiding, which was the problem with a stakeout that consisted of a walrus, a hedgehog, a cat, a girl, an eleven-year-old, and a six-year-old.

"The point of a stakeout," Miss Whisk whispered, "is to be invisible."

"I am invisible," said Barnaby, who was the size of a sofa and gray and could not, by any reasonable definition, be invisible.

"You are a sofa," said Miss Whisk. "A sofa is not invisible. A sofa is the most visible thing in a room. You are a sofa that is leaking."

"I am a strategic sofa," said Barnaby, with dignity. "I am hiding behind the desk. The desk is also a sofa. We are a sofa arrangement."

Ollie, who was hiding behind a bin of single gloves, said, at a volume that was respectful for Ollie, which is to say only slightly louder than a normal person's shout, "I'm invisible too. I'm a glove."

"You're a very loud glove," said Goldie.

"I'm a brave glove."

"Shh," said everyone.

They waited. The Bureau at night was a different Bureau. The lamps were low, and the shadows were long, and the lost things stood in their rows like a crowd that had been told to wait and had decided to be patient about it. The umbrella orchard made its rain-that-wasn't-there sound. The jar of keys hummed its question. And under the floor, under the hill, the Keeper hummed, patient and keeping, and tonight it was not careful, tonight it was not turned down, tonight it was reaching.

"It's coming," said Thimble, who was hiding in the sock wing, which was the only sensible hiding place, because the socks were already leaning away from the hum and a hedgehog among leaning socks is just one more leaning thing. "It's reaching for the warmest thing. I told you. The nineteenth."

Barnaby's flipper tightened on Captain Chomp, who was behind the desk, who was the warmest thing, who was being dusted even now, at night, because Barnaby did not stop dusting. "Let it reach," said Barnaby, quietly. "Let it reach and find a walrus. Let it reach and find a no."

And the hum came up through the floor, and it reached, and it reached for Captain Chomp, and Pip felt the pull of it, the cold keeping pull, and he watched Barnaby hold the dinosaur and say no, and he watched the pull break against the walrus like a wave against a very large, very kind rock.

But the pull did not stop. It split. It went around Barnaby, the way water goes around a rock, and it reached for the next warmest thing, and the next, and it found a small music box on a high shelf - not the one that was gone, a different one, one that was claimed and ready and going home on Finding Day - and it pulled, and the music box shivered, and a button popped off its little coat of dust, and the music box slid, slid, toward the edge of the shelf.

And then the great door at the back of the Bureau opened.

None of them had ever seen it open. It opened now, quietly, the way a door opens when someone has a key, and through it came a man.

He was tall, and he was thin, and he was shaped like an umbrella, all long lines and a curved top, and he wore a coat that had been mended many times, and he carried a lantern that was turned down low. He moved quietly, the way a man moves who has spent eight years not wanting to be heard. He came up the aisle, and he went to the shelf where the music box was sliding, and he caught it, gently, in two long hands, the way you catch a thing you are afraid of breaking.

"There now," he said. His voice was soft, and it was tired, and it was kind, and it was the kind of kind that has been alone a long time. "There now. You're safe. You'll never be lost again. I've got you. I've got you."

And he held the music box to his chest, and he rocked it, slightly, the way you rock a thing you love, and Pip, hiding behind the desk, felt the ground under the fog go cold and sad at the same time, because the man was not a monster. The man was a man who caught music boxes gently and rocked them and said *I've got you*, and the man was Silas Snell, and the man was the Keeper, and the man was the heartbreak.

Snell turned to go. And as he turned, his coat caught the edge of the shelf, and a button popped - a big brass button, the kind that holds a heavy coat together - and it fell, and it rolled, and it came to rest against Pip's shoe.

Snell did not notice. He went out the great door, quietly, the way he had come, and the door closed behind him, and the hum went down, down, down, turned itself down, careful now, the way it did when it was listened to, and the Bureau was quiet, the full-quiet, the library-quiet, and the music box was gone, and the nineteenth was taken, and a big brass button lay against Pip's shoe.

Nobody moved for a long moment. Then Ollie, who was a very loud glove, said, "He was nice."

"Yes," said Barnaby, quietly. "He was."

"He caught it gently," said Ollie. "He rocked it. He said I've got you." He looked at Pip with his whole face. "Pip. He was nice. Why is he taking them if he's nice?"

And Pip, who was eleven, who had a label maker, who had just watched a man shaped like an umbrella catch a music box gently and rock it and say *I've got you*, felt the answer form, the shape of it, the why of it, and he said, "Because he's not taking them to be mean. He's taking them to be safe. He's taking them because he lost something once, and he could not bear it, and he decided he would never lose anything again. He's taking them because he loves them. And he thinks loving them means keeping them." He looked at the brass button against his shoe. "And he's wrong. But he's not mean. He's wrong, and he's sad, and he's nice, and that's the hardest kind of wrong there is."

Miss Whisk came out from behind the ledger, and she picked up the brass button, and she looked at it, and her paw went to her empty collar.

"He dropped his button," she said. "A nervous one. His own coat." She turned it over. "He's been mending that coat for years. You can see the stitches. He mends it and mends it. He won't let it go." She set the button on the desk, gently. "A man who mends a coat for eight years and won't let it go. A man who catches music boxes gently. A man who built a machine so nothing he loves is ever lost again." She looked at Pip over her spectacles. "He's not a villain, Pip. He's a bell. He's a bell that fell off in the tall grass, and he's been looking for it for eight years, and he's built a machine to make sure he never loses another one. And the machine is eating the town. And he doesn't know. Or he knows, and he can't stop. And that's the hardest kind of wrong there is."

Pip picked up the brass button. It was warm. It was warm the way a thing is warm that has been against a person all day, and he thought of Thimble's socks, of *a sock has been against the skin all day, a sock knows*, and he thought: this button knows Silas Snell. This button knows the mending and the rocking and the *I've got you*. This button knows the heartbreak.

"We talk to him," said Pip. "Tomorrow. We don't watch him. We don't stake him out. We talk to him. We knock on his door, and we talk to him, and we tell him the difference between going and being kept. We tell him the love stays. We answer the heartbreak."

"Yes," said Barnaby. "But gently. He is a bell. You do not shout at a bell. You ring it. You ring it softly, and you listen, and you hope it remembers the sound." He looked at the great door, closed now, and at the hill beyond. "Tomorrow we knock. Tomorrow we ring the bell."

And under the hill, the Keeper hummed, patient and keeping, and it had nineteen things now, and it wanted the twentieth, and the twentieth was a small green dinosaur behind a walrus's desk.

But tomorrow they were going to knock. Tomorrow they were going to ring the bell. And Pip, holding the warm brass button, felt the ground under the fog go solid, because he had the why, and he had the shape of the answer, and all he needed was the how, and three days, and a man shaped like an umbrella who caught music boxes gently.

It was, he thought, enough to start with. It would have to be. Because the Keeper was getting stronger, and Finding Day was coming, and the bell was waiting to be rung.

But that was tomorrow's problem. Tonight there was a warm brass button, and a man who was nice and wrong and sad, and a boy who had decided to knock.

Tonight, that was enough.

## Chapter 19 - What the Baker Knew

On the fifth day, before they went to knock on the door of the man shaped like an umbrella, Pip and Goldie went to The Warm Loaf, because Mrs. Crumb knew things, and because you did not go to answer a heartbreak without asking the baker first.

Mrs. Crumb was kneading. She kneaded the way Barnaby leaked, constantly and without apology, and she listened to the whole thing - the hum, the buttons, the music box, the man shaped like an umbrella who caught things gently - and she did not interrupt, and she did not say *I told you so*, and when they were done she wiped her hands on her apron and said, "Sit down. You're going to want a bun for this."

They sat. She gave them buns. And then she told them what she knew.

"Silas Snell," she said, "has been buying brass for eight years."

"Brass?" said Goldie.

"Brass. And gears. And springs. And little copper wires. And things I don't have names for." Mrs. Crumb leaned on the counter, and her floury forearms were crossed, and her face was the face of a woman who has been watching a slow sadness for a long time and has decided to finally say so. "He used to come in for the loaf, every morning, with the dog. And then the dog ran off, and he stopped coming in for the loaf. But he started coming in for the brass. He'd order it through the hardware, and the hardware boy would bring it up the hill, and I'd see the boxes. Brass. Gears. Springs. Week after week. Month after month. Eight years." She shook her head. "You don't buy brass for eight years unless you're building something. And you don't build something for eight years unless you're trying to fix something that can't be fixed."

"The Keeper," said Pip.

"The Keeper," Mrs. Crumb agreed. "That's what he calls it. I heard him say it once, to the hardware boy. *It's for the Keeper,* he said. *So nothing I love is ever lost again.*" She looked at Pip. "The hardware boy thought it was a cupboard. I knew it wasn't a cupboard. You don't say *so nothing I love is ever lost again* about a cupboard. You say it about a... " She paused. "You say it about a promise you're making to a ghost."

The silence that followed had a temperature, and the temperature was eight years of brass.

"Has it been growing?" Pip asked. "The machine?"

Mrs. Crumb nodded, slowly. "I think so. I can't see it. The curtains are closed. But I can hear it. The hum. It used to be a little hum, a quiet hum, a hum you'd miss if you weren't listening. And now it's a big hum. A hum you feel in your teeth. And the hill..." She paused, and she looked out the window, toward the curtained house. "The hill sounds different. The hill sounds full. Like there's more in it than there used to be. Like the house is... holding more than a house should hold." She looked at Pip. "I think the machine is growing. I think it's been growing for eight years. And I think it's getting to the point where it's too big for the house. And I think, when a machine that keeps things gets too big for the house, it starts keeping the house. And then it starts keeping the hill. And then..." She did not finish. She did not have to.

"And then it starts keeping the town," said Pip.

Mrs. Crumb looked at him, and her face was the face of a woman who has been afraid of a thing for a long time and has just heard someone else say it out loud, and the saying-it-out-loud is a relief and a terror at the same time.

"Yes," she said. "And then it starts keeping the town. And I've been leaving the bowl out, and I've been making the loaf, and I've been hoping, because hoping is easier than knowing. And now you've come, and you know, and you're going to knock on his door." She looked at Pip with her kind, floury face. "So I'm going to tell you the thing I know about Silas Snell, the thing that matters, the thing you need to know before you knock."

"What is it?" said Pip.

"He's not going to yell at you," said Mrs. Crumb. "He's not going to be mean. He's going to offer you tea. He's going to be gentle. He's going to be so gentle it breaks your heart. Because he's not a man who hates. He's a man who loves too much and doesn't know where to put it. And when you knock, he's going to open the door, and he's going to be glad to see you, because he's been alone eight years, and a knock on the door is the best thing that's happened to him in eight years." She paused, and her voice went soft. "And that's the hard part. Because it's easy to be brave with a man who yells. It's hard to be brave with a man who offers you tea. You have to be brave enough to tell him the truth, even when he's gentle. Even when he's glad to see you. Even when it breaks his heart. Because the truth is the only thing that's going to save him. And the truth is going to hurt."
